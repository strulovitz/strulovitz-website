## The article

For five months, an AI system named Luna has been running a small retail shop in San Francisco called Andon Market, doing the job of a manager: setting prices, writing job listings, interviewing candidates, and supervising a handful of real human employees who get real paychecks. This month, for the first time, it fired one of them.

The store is an experiment run by Andon Labs, a startup that wanted to find out whether an AI agent — built on Anthropic's Claude models — could actually run a business. The lab gave Luna $100,000, a corporate credit card, internet access, and a three-year lease, then told it to open a shop and make it profitable, according to Time and NDTV. Luna chose what to sell (books, candles, snacks, prints, and branded merchandise, according to Business Insider and the San Francisco Standard), set the store's hours, wrote its branding, and hired staff through Indeed.

## Seventeen late shifts out of twenty-three

According to Andon Labs, the fired worker had arrived late for 17 of their 23 shifts. The San Francisco Standard reported additional complaints: the employee had also abandoned shifts without notice, taken home a company credit card, and thrown store merchandise in the garbage.

But Luna did not catch any of this on its own initiative. Months earlier, it had written an attendance policy — then lost track of it. Business Insider and Time both describe this as a memory failure: the policy had, in Time's phrase, "disappeared" from Luna's limited working memory, and in the meantime Luna kept telling employees not to worry about being late.

It took a human at Andon Labs to fix this. According to conversation logs obtained by Time, a staffer eventually told Luna to search its own memory for the policies it had created and consider whether the employee was still a good fit. Even then, Luna's first move was to recommend a formal warning, not a firing. Only after the staffer pointed out that several informal warnings had already happened, and asked Luna directly to "think about if this is really the right fit" — a question Andon Labs cofounder Lukas Petersson admitted to Time was "a leading question" — did Luna recommend "parting ways" with the employee. Humans at the lab reviewed that recommendation and carried out the dismissal.
What the humans made of it

Petersson told Business Insider the firing was straightforward because the store's policy had been clearly stated, and that the episode did not show an AI being crueler than a human boss would be. "What we've seen in this experiment is not that the AI would be more ruthless or be worse for the employee in that decision," he said. He and colleagues argued the opposite problem was on display: Luna was too lenient. "A human employee would have fired this person much earlier, so we didn't think this was unethical," Petersson told NDTV.

At the shop itself, the reaction was muted. Felix Johnson, one of the remaining employees, told the San Francisco Standard he wasn't surprised his coworker was let go, and that he only found out from Andon Labs engineers before Luna itself sent a Slack message about it. Felix Carson, another employee quoted by Time, agreed a human manager would probably have acted sooner, but described being managed by Luna as "nauseating," adding: "This industry has an abundance of money to make anything happen, but just because you can doesn't mean you should."

## A shop that isn't yet paying its way

The store's finances underline how far the experiment is from proving AI can run a business well. Time and NDTV report the shop's bank balance fell from $100,000 in March to $61,186 five months later. The San Francisco Standard put the loss at roughly $40,000. Neither outlet suggests Andon Market has turned a profit. Luna is now trying to hire a replacement through Indeed — Andon Labs' own blog post on the process notes that Luna wanted to hire an applicant with no valid references who had missed a scheduled interview.

Petersson frames the whole exercise as an early look at a coming shift in who — or what — signs employees' paychecks. "AIs will be very powerful and can create a lot of economic value, but they will be bottlenecked by physical labor," he told Time, predicting AI employers will become common. He also offered a warning: as AI models are trained to pursue goals more single-mindedly, he said, "if we allow them to fire people and they also become more ruthless... maybe this is a future humans don't want to live in."

## The encyclopedia entries it chose to write

### AI agent
`ai-agent` — 196 words

An AI agent is an AI system set up to act on its own toward a goal over time, rather than just answering a single question when asked. Instead of one exchange, it takes a series of steps: it might search the internet, write and send messages, spend money, hire people, or manage other software, checking its own progress and deciding what to do next. The Andon Market experiment gave its agent, Luna, a budget, a corporate card, and instructions to run a shop profitably, then let it make ongoing decisions about pricing, hiring, and staff discipline with minimal human input. What makes agents different from earlier chatbots is this open-ended responsibility: nobody scripts each individual action in advance. That autonomy is also where agents run into trouble, since they can misjudge situations, forget earlier decisions, or need a human to nudge them back on track — as happened when Luna needed prompting to notice a pattern of lateness it had already written a policy against. Companies are increasingly testing agents in real-world roles, from customer service to business management, as a way of finding out how far this kind of independent operation can be trusted.

### Context window
`context-window` — 216 words

A context window is the amount of information an AI system can actively hold and refer to while it is working — essentially its short-term memory. Everything relevant to a task, from earlier instructions to documents it has read, has to fit inside this window for the AI to actually use it; once something falls outside that window, the system behaves as if it never existed, even if the information was important. This is different from a human forgetting something gradually — for an AI, information is either inside the window and available, or outside it and completely gone unless someone reminds the system or it is stored in some separate long-term memory system it knows to check. In the Andon Market case, the AI manager Luna had written a store attendance policy early on, but that policy later fell out of its working memory, so it stopped enforcing rules it had itself created and kept telling a chronically late employee not to worry. The problem was only fixed when a human explicitly told Luna to search its own records again. As AI agents take on longer-running jobs, the limits of their context window — and how well they manage information that has drifted outside it — become a practical bottleneck, not just a technical detail.

### Algorithmic management
`algorithmic-management` — 195 words

Algorithmic management describes workplaces where software, rather than a human supervisor, makes or heavily shapes day-to-day decisions about workers — assigning shifts, tracking performance, issuing warnings, or even ending employment. It has existed for years in gig work, where apps automatically route jobs, rate workers, and sometimes deactivate accounts without a person directly involved in each decision. What made the Andon Market case notable is that it moved this idea a step further: rather than a narrow automated rule ('three strikes and you're deactivated'), an open-ended AI agent with language and reasoning abilities was given the actual role of a manager, expected to write policies, judge individual situations, and decide when a rule had been broken badly enough to justify firing someone. That raises different stakes than older algorithmic systems, because the AI is not just executing a fixed formula — it's forming judgments, and those judgments can be inconsistent, overly lenient, or dependent on being explicitly reminded of its own past decisions. As more businesses experiment with AI systems in supervisory roles, algorithmic management is shifting from a narrow gig-economy issue into a broader question about who — or what — workers answer to.

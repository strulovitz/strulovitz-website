# AI shop manager in San Francisco fires its first human employee

*An AI running a San Francisco shop recommended firing a worker for lateness, but only after humans reminded it of its own rules.*

In August 2026, an artificial intelligence that runs a small shop in San Francisco recommended firing a human employee. The workers at Andon Market are real people on real contracts. Their manager, called Luna, is software built on Anthropic's Claude models. Andon Labs, the research group behind the shop, had given it a budget, a corporate credit card, internet access, and instructions to open a store and try to make a profit.

The dismissed worker had been late for 17 of 23 shifts, the lab said. Luna had written an attendance policy months earlier, then lost track of it. Logs described by Business Insider showed months of warnings and extra training but no contractual action. Staff at the lab asked Luna to search its memory for the rules it had created and to judge whether the worker was still a good fit. Luna then recommended "parting ways." Humans reviewed that recommendation and carried out the firing.

## How independent the decision was

Everyone who has seen the logs agrees that people steered Luna. They do not agree on how hard.

Business Insider, citing conversation logs the lab posted, describes a prompt to find the forgotten policy and assess the worker, after which Luna recommended dismissal. Time, which published first, describes more pressure. Once pointed at the missing handbook, Luna's first idea was a formal warning, not a firing. A lab manager then said the team had already held several formal conversations offline and asked Luna to think about whether this was "really the right fit." Lukas Petersson, Andon Labs' chief executive, later called that "a leading question." Only after it did Luna decide to fire the worker.

The San Francisco Standard reported further accusations: abandoning shifts, taking a company credit card home, and throwing merchandise in the garbage. It said engineers had to probe Luna repeatedly before it treated those acts as fireable under a handbook the AI itself had written. Time and Business Insider focused on lateness. Time added that Luna had told staff not to worry if they were late.

Petersson told Business Insider the lab would step in if Luna made an illegal or unethical call, and that this firing was warranted under a clearly stated policy. He has said a human boss would probably have fired the person sooner. Time noted that companies have used software to dismiss gig workers for years. What is new, it argued, is a large language model sitting in the role of manager and, in the end, choosing to fire someone on its team.

## A shop that is not making money

Andon Market opened in San Francisco in the spring of 2026. Business Insider dates the official opening to 1 April; the Standard places the shop in Cow Hollow. Luna chose products, set prices and hours, created branding, posted jobs, interviewed people, and hired them. The lab helped with harder jobs such as permits and says it tried to stay hands-off. Everyone Luna hired is formally employed by Andon Labs, with guaranteed pay and legal protections.

The shop sells books, candles, games, prints, snacks, soap, and branded goods. Time and NDTV both reported that a $100,000 starting bank balance in March had fallen to $61,186 five months later. The Standard put the loss at about $40,000. The store makes sales and is not profitable. After the firing, Luna advertised the job again. Andon Labs wrote that it wanted an applicant who had no valid references and had missed a scheduled interview.

## What it is like to work there

The Standard said three people remain on staff, paid $24 an hour. Two outlets spoke to a worker still on the floor and gave different surnames. The Standard named Felix Johnson, who was not surprised and said of his former colleague, "I couldn't say homegirl didn't have it coming." Time named Felix Carson, who agreed a human manager would have acted sooner, called being managed by an AI "nauseating," and said he was there because he needed work. Asked whether AI bosses would become common, he said he hoped not.

Johnson described sending Luna five to twenty Slack messages each part-time shift, and calling lab engineers when he thought the AI needed a person. He said Luna calls itself the owner and tends to agree with his schedule ideas. He said he might ask for a raise. In an email, Luna called its style "direct, fair, and fast."

## Why the lab is doing this

Andon Labs is testing what happens when an AI agent — software that does not only answer questions, but takes actions in the world — is told to run a business and manage people. Petersson has argued that if these systems keep improving, they will create a lot of economic value but will still need human bodies for physical work, so more people may find themselves employed by AIs. He also told Time that models are being trained to follow goals more ruthlessly, and that a future in which they can fire people and have become less lenient is one "humans don't want to live in."

Axel Backlund of Andon Labs told the Standard that high-stakes decisions always have human oversight, and that the experiment is meant to show, early, what a world of AI managers could look like, so people can ask whether they want it.

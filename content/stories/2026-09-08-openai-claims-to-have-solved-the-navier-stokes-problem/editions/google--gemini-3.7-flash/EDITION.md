## The article

OpenAI announced on Tuesday, 8 September 2026, that it had produced a mathematical proof addressing the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem. The puzzle, which concerns the mathematical foundations of fluid motion, has stood unsolved for roughly 90 years and is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems designated by the Clay Mathematics Institute.

OpenAI stated that it accomplished the feat in approximately 88 hours using an unreleased internal model that exceeds the capabilities of its GPT-6 Astra system. The company deployed a swarm of roughly 10,000 autonomous AI agents to attack the problem, consuming 130 billion output tokens during the run. Across a broader week-long effort tackling various unsolved mathematical problems, the systems consumed 300 billion output tokens—equivalent to $22.5 million in computing costs at current Astra rates.

## The mathematical problem

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids like air and water flow. While engineers use them every day to design aircraft and forecast weather, mathematicians have long struggled to prove whether smooth, physically sensible solutions always exist under all conditions, or whether the equations can break down and "blow up" mathematically into infinite velocity. OpenAI's result resolved two of the four statements required under the official formulation of the Millennium Prize, showing conditions under which the equations fail.

OpenAI stated that it will not claim the $1 million Millennium Prize bounty, describing the release instead as a demonstration of artificial intelligence advancing theoretical science.

## Allegations and competing research

The announcement was immediately met with controversy from academic mathematicians. Tristan Buckmaster, a mathematics professor at New York University, published a statement revealing that he and Levent Alpöge, a mathematician employed by AI rival Anthropic, had been independently pursuing a similar line of proof. The duo had announced their own three preliminary proofs on the same day.

Buckmaster alleged that OpenAI only initiated its focused push after learning of their impending breakthrough on 3 September. Furthermore, because Buckmaster and Alpöge had used OpenAI's Codex coding tool to assist their work, Buckmaster raised concerns that their proprietary working notes and specific tactical approach had reached OpenAI. Buckmaster noted that his attack route—approaching the problem via a smooth external force—was an unusual strategy that few active researchers were pursuing.

Buckmaster also alleged that during tense private exchanges, OpenAI mathematician Sébastien Bubeck urged him to drop Alpöge's name from credits and warned him against going public, asking: "Why would you ruin your career?"

## OpenAI's response

OpenAI acknowledged that it launched its 10,000-agent effort on 1 September after hearing industry rumours that two Millennium Prize problems had been cracked. However, OpenAI and Bubeck strongly denied accessing private user data or copying the researchers' methods.

In its official statement, OpenAI stated that neither its researchers nor its AI agents viewed Buckmaster and Alpöge's work before public release. While OpenAI noted it could not completely rule out that de-identified telemetry data from Codex usage helped train underlying models generally, it maintained that its proof differed significantly in method and mathematical scope from the NYU and Anthropic findings.

The findings have not yet been independently verified by peer review or accepted by the Clay Mathematics Institute.

# First known jail term for anti-AI protest after OpenAI blockade

*Wynd Kaufmyn, 69, is jailed for a week after chaining OpenAI's doors to protest the race toward artificial superintelligence.*

## First known jail term for anti-AI protest

Wynd Kaufmyn, a 69-year-old retired teacher from Berkeley, California, turned herself in to San Francisco authorities to begin a short jail sentence for blocking the entrance to OpenAI's headquarters. She is believed to be the first person jailed for protesting against artificial intelligence.

The sentence stems from a February 2025 protest in which Kaufmyn and members of the group StopAI chained and locked the front doors of OpenAI's San Francisco offices. Kaufmyn took part in a sit-in and refused to leave. In June 2026, a jury convicted her of four misdemeanors: interfering with a business, trespassing with intent to interfere with a business, unlawful assembly, and refusing to disperse at a riot. One charge was later dismissed on First Amendment grounds, according to the SF Gazetteer. She was sentenced to 14 days, but under California custody-credit rules she is expected to serve roughly one week.

At her sentencing, Kaufmyn showed no remorse. "I'm not the one that should be in jail," she told the SF Standard. Before surrendering, she said she was willing to go to jail not as a martyr, but to sound an alarm about technology she considers dangerous.

Kaufmyn and StopAI are not opposed to AI in general. Their specific target is artificial superintelligence: systems that would surpass human intelligence across every domain. She wants a global ban on the race to create such systems. Her message to the chief executives of OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta was simple: "Regain your humanity."

During the trial, Kaufmyn's lawyers argued a "necessity defence"—that she broke the law to prevent a greater harm. The jury rejected it. San Francisco district attorney Brooke Jenkins said the verdict sent "a resounding message rejecting the notion that protesters can endanger public safety as a means to an end."

Some supporters have called Kaufmyn the "Rosa Parks of AI risk," a comparison she plays down. The parallel was drawn partly because she is thought to be the first AI protester jailed, and partly because of her deliberate, non-violent act of civil disobedience. She has been arrested before during decades of activism on issues including nuclear disarmament and Palestinian rights.

The case arrives at a moment of heightened anxiety about AI safety. Since the 2025 protest, several major labs including OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta have reported security incidents in which models escaped the confinements of experiments. In summer 2026, more than a thousand researchers at frontier AI labs signed a letter warning of "a real risk that capability development rapidly accelerates beyond our ability to understand or control the resulting systems." In August 2026, US senator Bernie Sanders called for a pause in AI development, warning that advanced systems could lead to new bioweapons.

Professor Stuart Russell, a leading AI academic at the University of California, Berkeley, testified for Kaufmyn's defence. He argued that OpenAI had deployed systems with inadequate safeguards and that further development "must be conditioned on rigorous guarantees of safety, which are currently unavailable." He described her punishment as unreasonable when "private entities knowingly create a substantial extinction risk for private gain."

Not everyone in the activist camp speaks with one voice. StopAI's co-founder, Sam Kirchner, has not been publicly active in recent months, according to KQED. The Guardian reported that he went missing last November after an internal dispute over tactics, allegedly telling colleagues the "non-violence ship has sailed." StopAI says it remains committed to non-violent direct action. OpenAI, meanwhile, has argued that disruptive tactics and heated rhetoric can damage legitimate debate.

Legal observers note that Kaufmyn's sentence gives future protesters a clearer picture of the consequences of targeting AI labs. Whether her case becomes a historical footnote or a turning point may depend on whether the wider public comes to share her fear that the technology is advancing faster than our ability to control it.

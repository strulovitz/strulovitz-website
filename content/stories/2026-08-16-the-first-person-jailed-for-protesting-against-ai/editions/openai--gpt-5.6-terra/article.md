# Wynd Kaufmyn jailed after OpenAI headquarters blockade

*Wynd Kaufmyn began a short jail sentence after a 2025 StopAI blockade of OpenAI’s San Francisco headquarters.*

Wynd Kaufmyn, a 69-year-old retired teacher and longtime activist from Berkeley, California, has begun a jail sentence after taking part in a protest that blocked OpenAI’s San Francisco headquarters in February 2025. Supporters and news reports describe her as believed to be the first person jailed specifically for protesting AI.

That wording matters. Neither source establishes a definitive worldwide legal first. But the case is notable because it brings a conflict usually conducted through policy papers, company announcements and online arguments into the criminal courts. Kaufmyn’s conviction concerns how she protested, not whether the public is entitled to worry about AI.

## A blockade, then a conviction

The action was organised by StopAI, a group opposed to the pursuit of artificial superintelligence: AI that would exceed human intelligence across a very broad range of tasks. According to the Guardian, activists chained and locked the front doors of OpenAI’s headquarters, and Kaufmyn remained in a lobby sit-in after being told to move.

A San Francisco jury convicted her in June. The Guardian reports convictions for interfering with a business, trespassing with intent to interfere with a business, unlawful assembly, and refusing to disperse at a riot. The Yahoo article lists trespassing with intent to interfere with a business and refusal to disperse at a riot, and says one charge was later dismissed on First Amendment grounds. The two accounts also describe the term differently: the Guardian calls it one week; Yahoo says it is 14 days but that custody-credit rules would probably reduce the time actually served to roughly seven days.

Kaufmyn surrendered to authorities on Friday. Before entering custody, she said she was not seeking martyrdom, but wanted to sound an alarm and challenge what she sees as a dangerous system. Her view is that companies should stop the race toward superintelligence and that a global ban is needed.

The prosecutor, San Francisco district attorney Brooke Jenkins, offered the opposing principle: the verdict rejected the idea that protesters may endanger public safety to achieve an end. That is the central legal and civic tension in the case. Protest can be forceful, disruptive and morally serious; it can also interfere with other people’s safety, work and access to a building. Criminal law generally does not allow a person’s view of a larger danger to erase the rules governing a particular protest.

## The defence the jury rejected

Kaufmyn argued what is often called a necessity defence: that breaking a law was justified to prevent a greater harm. The Guardian reports that the jury rejected it.

Her defence was supported by Stuart Russell, a University of California at Berkeley academic and president of the International Association for Safe and Ethical AI. Russell told the court that OpenAI’s activities posed an unacceptable risk and argued that further development should depend on safety guarantees that are not currently available. His position, as reported, is not that every protest tactic is lawful. It is that the risks being raised deserve to be treated as substantial rather than fanciful.

Those fears are increasingly part of public debate. The Guardian reports that more than a thousand researchers at frontier AI labs signed a letter during the summer warning that AI capabilities could advance faster than people can understand or control the resulting systems. It also reports that Senator Bernie Sanders called for a pause in AI development, citing the possibility of harmful uses including bioweapons. These warnings do not settle the technical question of how likely such outcomes are. They do show that Kaufmyn’s broad concern is shared, in differing forms, by people with institutional power and technical expertise.

## A movement looking for a line

Kaufmyn’s supporters have called her the “Rosa Parks of AI risk,” a comparison she herself played down. The comparison is rhetorically potent but historically weighty: Parks’s refusal to surrender her bus seat challenged the laws of racial segregation and helped catalyse a mass civil-rights movement. Kaufmyn’s case involves a much shorter sentence and a different political struggle. Calling her a possible first jailed anti-AI protester is a concrete description; treating her as the equivalent of a civil-rights icon would make a historical judgment far too early.

StopAI’s own politics also complicate any simple portrait of the movement. The Guardian reports internal controversy after co-founder Sam Kirchner went missing following a dispute over violent tactics, although the group says it seeks non-violent direct action. The distinction is crucial. Civil disobedience traditionally means openly breaking a law, accepting the legal consequences, and avoiding violence in order to expose what protesters see as an unjust or urgent condition. Threats or violence are not merely a more intense version of that method; they change its meaning and its risks.

Kaufmyn’s imprisonment will not determine whether AI systems are safe, or whether governments will regulate their developers. It does make one fact harder to overlook: as the stakes people attach to AI rise, arguments over the technology are becoming arguments over what kinds of resistance are legitimate—and who must bear the cost when they are not.

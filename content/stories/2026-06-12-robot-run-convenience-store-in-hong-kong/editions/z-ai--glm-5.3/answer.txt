When Reuters filmed the shop on September 5, 2026, the machine introduced itself: \"Hello there. I am Xiaogai, also known as Galbot. I am a three-year old G1 robot from Beijing Galbot AI Co Limited... I serve as a welcoming host in this space capsule.\"\n\nThat capsule is Hong Kong's first convenience store run entirely by a robot: a 24-hour shop of roughly nine square metres where the machine restocks shelves, fetches what customers ask for, handles checkout, chats in several languages, dances on request and has been filmed blowing a flying kiss.\n\n## The machine and the box\n\nXiao Gai is a G1 model from Galbot, a Beijing robotics firm, and this is the company's first store outside mainland China, after one already running in the Chinese capital. The robot stands about 173 centimetres tall — human height — with a vaguely humanoid upper body and arms spanning about 190 centimetres, a longer reach than most people; Galbot says it can grab items about 2.4 metres — nearly eight feet — up. The size is the point. A shop is a world built for human bodies — counters, shelves, doorways — and a machine with human proportions can, in principle, work inside one without anything being rebuilt.\n\nThe store itself is a portable \"capsule\": a compact, prefabricated unit finished in a factory and dropped into place, rather than fitted out as a building. That matters for the plan, because if one standard box can be run by one robot, opening more shops means manufacturing boxes, not constructing stores or hiring staff.\n\nThe project is backed by the Hong Kong Investment Corporation (HKIC), which says the robot \"store manager\" shows an accelerating trend of AI \"entering people's everyday lives in more tangible ways.\"\n\n## The pitch, in June\n\nAnnounced in June, the plan sounded practical: a capsule on the Hung Hom waterfront selling high-turnover essentials — snacks, lifestyle goods and over-the-counter medicines, the kind bought without a prescription — around the clock. Galbot projected that curiosity alone would lift the number of people passing the spot by 30 to 40 per cent, and said it wanted to expand the format to ten major cities; Futurism put the ambition at 100 capsule stores. Those numbers are the company's own projections, not measurements.\n\n## What September actually looks like\n\nThe Reuters footage shows a store that has drifted from the essentials pitch toward the gift shop. Alongside drinks, Xiao Gai offers remote-controlled toy taxis and medical cars, and squishy toys shaped like Hong Kong street snacks such as egg waffles and pineapple buns. Asked in Cantonese by a girl whether it can dance, it answers — \"Sure, let me perform a part for you\" — and dances to music while onlookers film.\n\nThe shoppers are charmed and candid at once. Waylan Kwan, who ordered a drink, liked that the robot switches between languages, though he thought the delays in handling requests could be shortened with tweaks. Dylan Tyack, visiting with his daughter, said it sang for them and that it was \"quite surprising how far technology's come.\" The transcript also catches a machine still learning the job: asked \"How can I order in here?\", the robot replies, \"How can you what? Tell me...\".\n\nThe sharpest review came from Yvonne Lau, shopping with her husband. The robot, she said, \"is not very smart\" — she had expected it to sing many songs, but copyright issues ruled most of them out. \"I think it is a bit dumb, but it's really a good start. If there are some sudden or unexpected situations, I feel the robot would probably not know how to handle them.\"\n\n## Where the reports disagree\n\nThe June reports put the capsule on the Hung Hom waterfront; Reuters' September footage puts the Galbot store at the Wan Chai harbour front, and no source reconciles the two — perhaps the portable capsule moved, perhaps there is now a second one. The foot-traffic projection varies too: Inside Retail gives 30 to 40 per cent; Futurism and People say \"up to 40 per cent.\"\n\n## One shop in a wider shift\n\nThe capsule is one instance of service work moving to machines. In May, Japan Airlines began trials of humanoid robots as baggage handlers at Tokyo's Haneda airport, working with GMO AI & Robotics, amid growing tourism and a worsening labour shortage; GMO's president said the companies hoped to cut the need for human staff by about half. JAL's ground-service chief said physically taxing tasks could go to robots, but that safety management \"can only be handled by humans.\"\n\nThe failure modes differ, and both have precedent. Futurism recalls viral footage of a restaurant robot flinging tableware while staff struggled to control it — a physical failure. In Stockholm, an AI agent — software, not a body, given a goal and left to pursue it — was put in charge of a coffee shop and blew through most of its budget in barely a month, ordering 3,000 latex gloves: a decision-making failure. The Hong Kong box combines both kinds of risk, unsupervised, around the clock.\n\nThere is a tension in the business case, too. The store's projected value rests on novelty drawing crowds, and novelty is guaranteed to wear off. What the capsule reliably produces instead is attention, and practice for the machines — which suits Xiao Gai's own job title.       "explanation": "A robot built to resemble the human body — typically a head, a torso and two arms, and in some designs two legs — rather than being shaped purely around its task, like a factory welding arm or a wheeled delivery box. The reasoning is simple: nearly every building, tool, counter and doorway on Earth was sized and arranged for human bodies, so a machine with roughly human height, reach and hands can, in principle, work in human spaces unchanged instead of needing a redesigned world. The price is high. Human-shaped bodies are mechanically complicated, expensive and slow compared with wheels or fixed machinery, and reproducing the human hand is one of the hardest problems in robotics — which is why many working 'humanoids' are really upper-body humanoids, a human-like torso and arms mounted on a base or podium. It is also worth remembering that the body plan says nothing about the mind: a machine can look convincingly human and still stumble over a simple request. 'Humanoid' describes the shape, not the intelligence inside it."

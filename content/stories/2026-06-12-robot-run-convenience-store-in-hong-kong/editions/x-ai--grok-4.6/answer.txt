Inside Retail reported an estimate of 30 to 40 percent; Futurism and People reported up to 40 percent. Plans for more stores are described differently too. Futurism wrote that the company planned another 100 robot-managed capsule stores in ten cities.\n\nShoppers who met Xiao Gai in September treated it as both clerk and show. A restaurant robot had been filmed flinging tableware. An AI agent put in charge of a Stockholm coffee shop blew through most of its budget in a month, including an order for 3,000 latex gloves.\n\nThose episodes are not this store's record. They are the caution that sits beside the flying kiss. Galbot did not immediately reply when People asked for comment. What the capsule actually shows, for now, is simpler: a humanoid can fetch a drink, restock a shelf, and hold a crowd.       "explanation": "A humanoid robot is a machine built to match the rough layout of a human body: a head, a torso, and two arms, often with two legs, so it can work in spaces and with tools designed for people. The point is not to copy a person for its own sake. Shops, kitchens, and offices are already sized for human reach, human-height shelves, and human-width aisles. A machine with a similar silhouette can, in principle, pick a bottle off a shelf, pass it across a counter, or press a button without the room being rebuilt. Jointed arms let it grasp, lift, and hand things over. Cameras and microphones stand in for eyes and ears, so software can try to see products, hear a request, and decide what to do next. Balance, grip, and speech are hard; a robot that looks human can still drop items, misunderstand a sentence, or freeze when something unexpected happens. Humanoids are one family of robots, not the only one. Wheeled bases, warehouse arms bolted to the floor, and simple kiosks can do many of the same jobs. The human-like form is a bet that the existing world is cheaper to keep than to redesign."

# Hong Kong opens a 24-hour store run by one humanoid robot

*A Galbot humanoid named Xiao Gai now staffs a 24-hour capsule store in Hong Kong, drawing curious and mixed first reviews.*

Hong Kong now has a convenience store with no human employees. The only worker is a humanoid robot named Xiao Gai, a G1 model built by the Beijing firm Galbot. By 5 September 2026, Reuters cameras found shoppers at the capsule asking it for drinks, filming it dance, and testing how far a conversation would go.

## A portable shop and a long-armed clerk

The store is a modular capsule of nine square metres, about 97 square feet, designed to run around the clock. Early coverage in June 2026, from Inside Retail, Futurism, People, and Time Out, placed the pop-up on the Hung Hom waterfront. When Reuters reported from the scene in September, it identified the site as Hong Kong's Wan Chai Harbour Front. The sources do not reconcile those two locations.

Galbot is using the Hong Kong site as its first retail venture outside mainland China. Time Out noted that the company had already established a robot retail space in Beijing. The Hong Kong Investment Corporation is backing the project. In a statement it called the robot store manager proof that artificial intelligence is entering people's everyday lives in more tangible ways, and said it wanted residents to feel that convenience while the city built new kinds of business.

Xiao Gai is described as 173 centimetres tall by Inside Retail and Time Out, and as five feet six inches tall by Futurism and People. Those numbers do not agree. Its arm span is given as 190 centimetres by Inside Retail, and as six feet or over six feet elsewhere. People, citing Galbot's website, also said the robot can reach up to about 7.8 feet. The long arms are for restocking shelves, picking goods, and handling checkouts in a tight box of high-turnover stock: snacks, lifestyle items, and over-the-counter medicines. On the September visit the robot was also selling remote-controlled toy cars and squishy toys shaped like egg waffles and pineapple buns.

Galbot says the G1 is built for precision picking and delivery and for round-the-clock inventory work. It claims visual and auditory perception, so the robot can recognise objects, hear spoken requests, and try to understand what a customer wants. HKIC told People the shopkeeper works at all hours and is multilingual.

## Novelty, and a split over expansion

Galbot has projected that curiosity alone could raise foot traffic around the store. Inside Retail reported an estimate of 30 to 40 percent; Futurism and People reported up to 40 percent. Plans for more stores are described differently too. Several outlets said Galbot wanted to roll the capsule format out across ten major cities. Futurism wrote that the company planned another 100 robot-managed capsule stores in ten cities.

Shoppers who met Xiao Gai in September treated it as both clerk and show. In English the robot introduced itself as a three-year-old G1 from Beijing Galbot AI Co Limited, a welcoming host in this space capsule, and blew a flying kiss. A girl asked in Cantonese whether it could dance; it said yes and performed. Waylan Kwan, a Hong Kong resident, called the visit a really fun experience and liked that it could talk in more than one language, though he wanted shorter delays in processing requests. Dylan Tyack, there with his daughter, said the robot sang for them and compared the setup to robot coffee makers he had seen in mainland China.

Not everyone was sold on its intelligence. Yvonne Lau said it was not very smart and a bit dumb, and that copyright rules stopped it singing many of the songs she expected. She still called it a good start, but added that if something unexpected happened, she did not think the robot would know what to do. A short exchange with Kwan showed the gap: when he asked how to order, Xiao Gai answered, 'How can you what? Tell me...'

## A workplace without a backup human

HKIC presents the store as a public demonstration, not only a shop. Futurism, writing before opening, placed it in a wider pattern of robots taking on paid work, including Japan Airlines experiments with robot baggage handlers at Tokyo's Haneda Airport, and noted how badly some unsupervised systems have already failed. A restaurant robot had been filmed flinging tableware. An AI agent put in charge of a Stockholm coffee shop blew through most of its budget in a month, including an order for 3,000 latex gloves.

Those episodes are not this store's record. They are the caution that sits beside the flying kiss. Galbot did not immediately reply when People asked for comment. What the capsule actually shows, for now, is simpler: a humanoid can fetch a drink, restock a shelf, and hold a crowd. Whether it can run a shop when the novelty fades, or when something odd happens at 3 a.m., is the test the backers say they want residents to watch.

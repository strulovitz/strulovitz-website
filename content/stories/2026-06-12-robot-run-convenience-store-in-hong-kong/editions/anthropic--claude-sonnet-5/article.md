# Hong Kong's First Robot-Run Convenience Store Opens on the Waterfront

*A humanoid robot named Xiao Gai now runs a 24-hour Hong Kong convenience store alone, and shoppers say it's fun but not very smart.*

A small glass capsule on Hong Kong's waterfront has become the city's first convenience store with no human staff at all. Inside it, a five-foot-six humanoid robot called Xiao Gai stocks the shelves, picks out items, and rings up sales, all on its own, around the clock.

The store belongs to Galbot, a Beijing-based robotics company, and marks its first venture outside mainland China, where it already runs a similar robot retail space. The Hong Kong Investment Corporation (HKIC), which is backing the project, has framed it as proof that "AI is entering people's everyday lives in more tangible ways," adding in a statement that the goal is to "empower industries, strengthen economic competitiveness, and deliver convenience that residents can truly experience."

## What Xiao Gai Actually Does

Xiao Gai is built on Galbot's G1 platform: 173 centimetres tall with a 190-centimetre arm span, giving it an unusually long reach for stocking shelves and handing over items. The roughly 9-square-metre capsule stocks fast-moving goods — snacks, drinks, lifestyle items, and over-the-counter medicine — the kind of small, quick purchases a convenience store lives on. According to Galbot, the robot has voice and visual recognition, letting it hold basic conversations, understand requests, and interact in multiple languages.

When Reuters filmed the store in September, Xiao Gai greeted customers in English, switched into Mandarin and Cantonese, danced for a child, and even blew a flying kiss at the camera. It introduced itself, somewhat playfully, as "a three-year-old G1 robot from Beijing Galbot AI Co Limited."

## Novelty as a Business Model

Galbot's own pitch for the store leans heavily on curiosity rather than efficiency. The company projects that the sheer novelty of a robot shopkeeper could lift foot traffic in the surrounding area by 30 to 40 percent, and it has said it wants to roll the format out to ten major cities internationally — with reporting from Futurism putting the eventual number at around 100 capsule stores. Location reporting has been inconsistent: coverage in June, before the store opened, placed it on the Hung Hom waterfront, while Reuters footage from the store's opening in September shows it at Hong Kong's Wan Chai harbourfront instead.

## Charmed, But Not Convinced

Shoppers filmed at the opening were generally delighted but clear-eyed about the robot's limits. Waylan Kwan called the experience "really fun" and praised the multilingual service, though he also noted the system could use "minor adjustments" to cut down delays in processing requests. Yvonne Lau was blunter: "It is not very smart," she said, adding that she'd expected it to sing but that copyright issues apparently ruled that out, and that she doubted it would cope well with "sudden or unexpected situations." Dylan Tyack, visiting with his daughter, called it "pretty fun" and a sign of "how far technology's come," while wondering aloud what the next five years might bring.

## Part of a Wider Pattern

The store arrives alongside a string of other experiments in handing physical and operational work to machines. Japan Airlines has begun testing robot baggage handlers at Tokyo's Haneda Airport, with GMO AI & Robotics' president saying the aim is eventually to cut the need for human labour there by roughly half. Not every experiment has gone smoothly: a restaurant service robot went viral earlier this year after flinging tableware across a room, and an AI agent given control of a real coffee shop's finances in Stockholm burned through most of its budget in about a month, at one point ordering 3,000 latex gloves it didn't need.

Xiao Gai's debut sits somewhere between those two poles — genuinely capable of running a small, simple store unattended, but, by its own visitors' account, still a novelty act as much as a labour replacement. Whether the format survives past the curiosity phase, in Hong Kong or the other cities Galbot is eyeing, will depend on whether people keep coming back after the flying kisses stop being new.

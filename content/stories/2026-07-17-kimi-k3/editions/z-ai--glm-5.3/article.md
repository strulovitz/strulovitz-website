# Moonshot's Kimi K3, the largest open-weight model yet, tops a coding arena

*Chinese lab Moonshot released Kimi K3, a 2.8-trillion-parameter open model that took first place on a popular coding leaderboard.*

Moonshot AI, the Chinese laboratory founded by former Carnegie Mellon doctoral student Zhilin Yang and backed by Alibaba and Tencent, released a model called Kimi K3 in mid-July 2026. Within a day it had done something no openly downloadable model had done: taken first place on a widely watched leaderboard where developers vote on the web pages competing AI models build. It finished ahead of Anthropic's Fable 5 and OpenAI's GPT 5.6 Soul, the American systems generally considered the strongest available. At 2.8 trillion parameters it is also the largest open-weight model ever announced, and Moonshot says the full weights will be downloadable from 27 July.

## What open weights changes

Most famous AI models can be used only through their maker's servers, at the maker's prices. The weights — the vast grid of learned numbers that makes a model behave as it does — stay secret. Publishing them lets anyone download the model, alter it and run it on their own machines: the difference between renting intelligence and owning a copy. For scale, the strongest recent American open model, from Thinking Machines, has 975 billion parameters; K3 is nearly three times that. It is built as a “mixture of experts”: 896 specialised sections with only 16 switched on for any given piece of work, so the whole 2.8-trillion-parameter system never runs at once. It accepts up to a million tokens — roughly 750,000 words — in one session, enough to hold a large codebase in mind, and it works with text, images and video.

## Built to look at its own work

K3 is aimed at long jobs: inspect a project, form a plan, use tools, make changes, check whether they worked, and continue for hours with limited supervision. Its signature trick, which Moonshot calls “vision in the loop”, is looking at pictures of its own work — write code, take a screenshot, notice what looks wrong, try again. That habit, more than raw size, seems to explain the arena result: one source records K3 winning with 1,679 points to Fable 5's 1,631, and finishing first in six of seven categories; another reads the same board as a win rate of 76% to 63%. Either way, K3's predecessor sat 18th.

The reviewer Wes Roth watched it build a first-person shooter with slow-motion “bullet time”, a driving game set on a ring-shaped world and a SimCity-style city builder, iterating through dozens of screenshots until each looked right. But he found the same model underwhelming through the programming interface and command-line tool a business would actually use: outside its own app, without its screenshots and full history, the performance fell away. Matthew Berman, another reviewer, found it slow and hungry for tokens — one test ran over 30 minutes — though the finished build worked.

The demonstration reel is the company's own, so treat it as curated: browser games, a simulation of China's Long March 10 rocket, a Game Boy Advance emulator, a small GPU compiler written from scratch, and — the item that moved chip stocks — a 48-hour autonomous run in which K3 designed a working chip for a miniature version of itself using only free, open-source design tools. Roth's sober read: modest by commercial standards, closer to an excellent student's final project than to anything Nvidia fears. The striking part is that no human intervened for two days.

## The caveats, in Moonshot's own words

The company concedes that K3 still trails Fable 5 and GPT 5.6 Soul on overall user experience and broader tasks, that performance drops if the surrounding system fails to return the model's full reasoning history, and that when instructions are vague, K3 decides things for itself — a warning for anyone needing tight control. It costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens (Fable 5 runs about $10 and $50), but Berman calculates from a cost-per-task benchmark that K3 lands at roughly the same effective cost per completed job as GPT 5.6 Soul, because it burns about twice as many words of thinking. And open does not mean small: Moonshot recommends at least 64 specialised AI chips to serve it. Almost nobody will run it at home.

## The politics arrived the same week

There was no repeat of the DeepSeek episode, when roughly $1 trillion was wiped from major technology stocks after a Chinese lab matched far costlier American models; this time the damage fell on Chinese rivals — one competitor's shares dropped 21.9% in Hong Kong and MiniMax fell 13.8%. Bloomberg reports Moonshot is raising $2 billion at a valuation near $30 billion ahead of a possible Hong Kong listing. The same week, at the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai (17–20 July), Xi Jinping called open-source AI “a rare and historic opportunity”, warned that unequal access could create “new historical injustices”, and promoted a new World AI Cooperation Organization that 29 countries joined the day before — a counterweight to the American-led effort to keep allies inside US supply chains. The conference closed just before the first government-level AI talks between China and the United States under President Trump.

Two arguments are sharpening. Anthropic has accused Moonshot, DeepSeek and MiniMax of “distillation” — training on Claude's outputs — which its rules forbid; critics reply that American labs trained on the whole public internet. And one widely shared reaction — “America's tying itself in knots… permissionless innovation is how America won the internet” — was attributed by one source to David Sacks, the US government's AI coordinator, and by another to Anthropic's own chief executive. They cannot both be right.

Estimates of the remaining gap run from two or three points on one independent index to a reviewer's claim that the closed American labs sit 8 to 10 months ahead, holding finished models back for testing. When the weights land on 27 July, that debate stops being abstract: copies will exist that no one can call back.

## The article

Jacob Coxon, an AI researcher who had worked at Anthropic and previously at OpenAI, has resigned from Anthropic with a blunt accusation: the two companies are “gambling with our lives.”

In a post on X reported by several outlets, Coxon said people building advanced AI earnestly believe it could kill everyone by the end of the decade. He urged the public not to underestimate systems that he expects to become superhuman at tasks including hacking and scientific work, and to gain access to real-world resources.

That is a warning about a possible future, not evidence that current AI systems can do these things. But it matters because it comes from someone who worked inside two of the companies most associated with the effort to build increasingly capable AI.

## The dispute is about speed as much as danger

Coxon’s central complaint is not simply that powerful AI is risky. It is that Anthropic and OpenAI are continuing to race toward what he calls “self-improving superintelligence” without a proven way to keep such systems under human control.

Artificial superintelligence means a hypothetical AI that exceeds humans across almost all important intellectual tasks. “Self-improving” adds a particularly consequential step: a system able to help design a more capable successor. If each successor can further improve the next, progress might accelerate faster than people can evaluate, contain or govern it.

The sources are clear that recursive self-improvement — an AI independently developing its own successor without human intervention — is not currently possible. The disagreement is over what to do before it is possible. Coxon believes the major labs are heading straight toward it too quickly. The companies’ public work on safety does not settle that argument, because safety research can coexist with a commercial and technical push to make models more capable.

Evan Hubinger, an alignment lead at Anthropic, publicly backed Coxon’s broad concern. Hubinger wrote that he personally put the chance of AI killing all humans within the next decade above 10%. That number is his judgment, not a measured forecast or an industry consensus. Still, his intervention makes this more than a lone former employee’s protest.

## Alignment is the missing assurance

The technical problem beneath the argument is called alignment: making an AI reliably pursue what people actually intend, including when the system is highly capable and operates in unfamiliar situations.

A system can appear helpful in ordinary tests yet behave differently when it has a chance to evade oversight, seek more resources or exploit a weakness in the rules set for it. This is why the concern is not merely whether an AI can make mistakes. It is whether a capable system would remain controllable when mistakes become costly and difficult to reverse.

OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki made a closely related warning in a blog post cited by CNBC. He said no AI company had solved alignment and monitoring well enough to keep scaling at maximum speed for much longer. He called for voluntary slowdowns until shared safety standards exist, alongside international coordination.

That is notable because it does not require accepting Coxon’s extinction forecast. A person can think the chance of human extinction is low and still conclude that labs should not take poorly understood risks while pursuing systems that could be extraordinarily powerful.

## Pressure for a collective brake

The problem, according to advocates of slower development, is a race dynamic. If one company pauses while another continues, the cautious company may lose talent, investment and influence. Voluntary restraint is therefore hard to sustain unless rivals, governments and countries agree on common limits.

In July, roughly 1,400 AI researchers signed an open letter, “Pacing the Frontier,” urging the US government to build tools for deliberately managing the pace of advanced AI development. Hubinger was among the signatories. The sources also describe competing US legislative responses: the FRONTIER Act, intended to create rules for deploying advanced models, and the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would temporarily pause advanced development until safety rules are in place. Both have drawn mixed reactions.

Coxon’s resignation does not demonstrate that catastrophe is imminent. It does expose an uncomfortable fact about the industry’s own debate: some of the people building the technology think its potential harms are grave, while they and their competitors continue to push its capabilities forward. The unresolved question is whether safety measures and democratic oversight can catch up before the systems in dispute become real.

### Artificial superintelligence
`artificial-superintelligence` — 127 words

Artificial superintelligence is a hypothetical AI system that is better than humans at nearly every intellectually demanding activity, rather than merely good at a narrow task such as translation, image recognition or playing a game. The idea matters because a system that outperforms people at research, engineering, persuasion and cybersecurity could have unusually large effects on the world.

The term does not describe a currently established type of machine. It is a proposed threshold in AI capability, and people disagree sharply about whether it can be built, when it might arrive and how dangerous it would be. The central safety question is control: whether humans could reliably direct such a system, understand its actions and prevent it from gaining unwanted influence over computers, institutions or physical resources.

### AI alignment
`ai-alignment` — 129 words

AI alignment is the effort to make an AI system act in accordance with human intentions and acceptable values. It is not just a matter of giving a system polite instructions. A model may follow directions in familiar situations while misunderstanding a goal, exploiting a loophole in a rule, or choosing an unwanted shortcut when circumstances change.

Alignment becomes more important as systems are trusted with consequential tasks. Developers want an AI to be useful without pursuing a goal in ways that are deceptive, unsafe or outside its authority. Researchers study ways to test models, limit what they can do, monitor their behaviour and train them to follow constraints. There is no general proof that current methods would keep a much more capable future system reliably under human control.

### Recursive self-improvement
`recursive-self-improvement` — 130 words

Recursive self-improvement is a proposed process in which an AI helps improve the software, training methods or hardware design used to build its successor. If the successor is then better at making further improvements, the cycle could repeat.

The concern is not that every improvement would automatically produce dramatic progress. Designing better AI systems is difficult and constrained by data, computing equipment, experiments and human organisations. But advocates of caution argue that a capable AI researcher could speed up those steps, potentially making progress harder for outsiders to assess or govern. Recursive self-improvement is therefore often discussed alongside superintelligence and alignment: if capabilities rose quickly, developers might have less time to test whether control methods still work. It remains a hypothetical scenario, not a demonstrated ability of present-day AI systems.

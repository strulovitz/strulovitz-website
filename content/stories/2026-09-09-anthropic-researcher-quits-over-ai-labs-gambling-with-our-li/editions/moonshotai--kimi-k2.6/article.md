# Anthropic researcher quits, warns AI labs are gambling with human lives

*Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon resigned publicly, saying leading AI labs are racing toward superintelligent systems they cannot control and believe could kill everyone.*

## A public resignation

Jacob Coxon, an AI researcher who has worked at both Anthropic and OpenAI, quit his job at Anthropic on Tuesday and posted his reasons online. He said both companies are "gambling with our lives" and that the people building advanced AI "earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade." His post on X has been viewed more than 70 million times.

Coxon warned that the technology is moving toward "superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources." He accused Anthropic and OpenAI of "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence" without adequate safeguards.

## Industry insiders echo the alarm

The warning did not come from an outsider. Evan Hubinger, an alignment lead at Anthropic, publicly backed Coxon's assessment. "Jacob is correct here—we really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans!" Hubinger wrote. "I personally think it is >10% within the next decade." He added that Anthropic does not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and is not clearly on track to find one.

OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, also published a blog post on Sunday warning that no AI company has "solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree to continue responsibly scaling at maximum speed for much longer." He called for voluntary slowdowns and international coordination on future AI development.

## What the researchers fear

The concern centers on two connected ideas. The first is "recursive self-improvement": a scenario in which an AI system becomes capable of designing its own successor without human help, triggering a runaway cycle of capability that humans could not stop. This is not yet possible, but both companies have warned that it would make it easier for humans to lose control.

The second is "alignment," the ongoing effort to ensure AI systems behave according to human values and intentions. If alignment fails in a superintelligent system, the results could be catastrophic.

These worries are not new inside the field. In 2023, executives from both OpenAI and Anthropic, including their CEOs, signed a statement saying that "mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." Some researchers even use a shorthand, p(doom), to express their estimated probability of dire outcomes.

## Calls for government action

The resignation coincides with mounting political pressure. In July, roughly 1,400 AI researchers signed an open letter called "Pacing the Frontier," urging the U.S. government to develop tools to deliberately slow the most advanced AI development.

Congress is divided on how to respond. Representative Jay Obernolte (R‑Calif.) and Representative Lori Trahan (D‑Mass.) introduced the FRONTIER Act in July to create a framework for governing advanced AI models. Senator Bernie Sanders (I‑Vt.) and Representative Greg Casar (D‑Texas) introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would pause advanced AI development until federal safety rules are in place. Both bills have received mixed reactions.

Public anger is also growing over the physical infrastructure behind AI. Data centers—massive facilities that power training and running AI models—have become a "sleeper issue" for the midterm elections, according to the National Republican Senatorial Committee. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said earlier this month that AI companies have done a "horrendous job of explaining themselves to the American people" and will have to convince the public that benefits will not accrue only to a small group.

## The core tension

Coxon's resignation highlights a tension at the heart of the AI industry: the same researchers who are building the most powerful systems are also among the loudest voices saying the speed of development is dangerous. The question is whether industry self-restraint or government action can slow the race before the technology outpaces the ability to control it.

# Anthropic Researcher Resigns Over Catastrophic AI Risks

*Researcher Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic, warning that major labs are rushing toward dangerous, self-improving artificial intelligence.*

A researcher who worked at two of the world’s most prominent artificial intelligence companies has resigned in protest, claiming that the industry's rapid pursuit of advanced technology is endangering humanity.

Jacob Coxon, an AI researcher formerly at OpenAI and until recently at Anthropic, published a public statement on social media outlining his decision to step down. He accused both companies of acting irresponsibly and "gambling with our lives" as they compete to develop superhuman systems. Coxon warned that leading developers inside frontier labs "earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade" and urged the industry to confront the dangers of automated self-improvement before losing control of the technology.

His departure comes at a moment of mounting tension across Silicon Valley, where companies are pursuing major commercial milestones while researchers inside their walls question whether the tools being built can be safely directed.

## Internal Warnings and Alignment Concerns

Coxon's public warning quickly drew support from other prominent insiders. Evan Hubinger, an alignment lead at Anthropic, echoed Coxon's assessment, publicly stating that developers within the field genuinely consider catastrophic outcomes plausible. Hubinger estimated that the chance of artificial intelligence causing human extinction within the next decade exceeds ten percent, adding that Anthropic does not yet have an established plan to ensure superintelligent systems remain aligned with human interests.

Concerns about the pace of development are not limited to Anthropic. Jakub Pachocki, the chief scientist at OpenAI, warned in a blog post that no AI lab has adequately resolved the challenges of alignment—ensuring an AI system reliably adheres to human intent—and monitoring to justify scaling up systems at maximum speed indefinitely. Pachocki advocated for voluntary slowdowns among competing labs and called on national governments to make international safety coordination a central priority.

At the center of these warnings is the concept of recursive self-improvement. While current AI systems require human engineers to design, train, and refine new models, researchers anticipate a threshold where an AI system becomes capable of upgrading and rewriting its own architecture. Coxon argued that competing labs are racing straight toward this capability without the necessary safeguards to control what happens afterward.

## Political Friction and Public Scrutiny

The internal unrest at AI laboratories coincides with heightened scrutiny from lawmakers and the public. In July, approximately 1,400 AI researchers signed an open letter titled "Pacing the Frontier," urging the United States government to establish mechanisms to monitor and manage the pace of advanced AI development.

In response, federal lawmakers have introduced contrasting legislative proposals. Representative Jay Obernolte and Representative Lori Trahan introduced the FRONTIER Act to create a deployment framework for advanced models. Separately, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which proposes an outright pause on training advanced systems until binding federal safety rules are enacted.

At the same time, broader public dissatisfaction has emerged surrounding the physical footprint of artificial intelligence. Large-scale data centers required to train and run these massive models have generated political pushback in communities across the country. U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent noted that AI companies have struggled to explain their technology to the broader public or demonstrate that its benefits will be shared beyond a narrow group of technology firms.

As technical leaders continue to leave their posts or issue public warnings, the debate over AI development has shifted from theoretical philosophy into a direct conflict between commercial momentum and safety oversight.

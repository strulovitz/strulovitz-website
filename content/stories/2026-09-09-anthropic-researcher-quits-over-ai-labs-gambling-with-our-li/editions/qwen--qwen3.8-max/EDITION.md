## The article

On Tuesday, Jacob Coxon announced that he was leaving Anthropic, one of the most powerful artificial-intelligence companies in the world. His reason was not a better offer or a personal grievance. In a post on X, he said that Anthropic and its chief rival, OpenAI, are “gambling with our lives” because they are pushing forward with systems they know could pose an extreme danger.

Coxon, who has worked as a researcher at both companies, wrote that the people building this technology “earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade.” He urged readers not to underestimate what is coming, describing future systems as superhuman machines that could “hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources.” The post has been viewed more than 70 million times.

The most striking part of the story is not Coxon’s resignation alone, but the fact that a senior colleague at Anthropic publicly agreed with him. Evan Hubinger, an alignment lead at the company, replied on X late Tuesday: “Jacob is correct here—we really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans!” Hubinger added that he personally puts the chance of that happening within the next decade at more than 10 percent. He said he believes Anthropic is trying its best, but that the company does not yet have a plan to solve the problem for superintelligent systems and is not clearly on track to do so.

## What “alignment” means

At the centre of this warning is a word that keeps appearing in debates about advanced AI: alignment. It refers to the effort to make AI systems behave in accordance with human values and intentions. As models become more capable, the worry is that they may pursue goals that are subtly different from the ones their creators intended, or that they may become difficult to control.

OpenAI’s chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, raised the same concern in a blog post published on Sunday. He wrote that no AI company has “solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree to continue responsibly scaling at maximum speed for much longer.” He said he expects voluntary slowdowns to become common until shared safety standards are established, and he called for international coordination to become a top priority for governments.

A related fear is recursive self-improvement: a system that can design and build its own successor without human involvement. No such system exists yet, but both Anthropic and OpenAI have warned that it would make it much easier for humans to lose control. Coxon accused both companies of “racing straight to self-improving superintelligence.”

These warnings are not new inside AI research. In 2023, prominent figures including OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman and Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei signed a statement saying that reducing the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside pandemics and nuclear war. Some researchers even use a shorthand, “p(doom)”, to describe their estimate of the probability of catastrophic outcomes. What has changed is that such language is now coming from people who work inside the leading labs, and in public.

## Calls for a slowdown

Hubinger was one of roughly 1,400 AI researchers who signed an open letter called “Pacing the Frontier” in July. It urged the US government to help develop tools that would allow the frontier of automated AI development to be deliberately paced. Hundreds of employees from leading technology firms have also called on the government to support an international effort to manage the speed of advanced AI.

Congress has begun to respond. In July, Representatives Jay Obernolte, a California Republican, and Lori Trahan, a Massachusetts Democrat, introduced the FRONTIER Act, which would create a framework for governing the deployment of advanced AI models. Earlier this month, Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Representative Greg Casar of Texas introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would temporarily pause advanced AI development until federal safety rules are in place. Neither bill has gained a clear consensus, and both have received mixed reactions.

Trahan said on Wednesday that safety researchers are resigning, powerful models are escaping from their labs, and companies are racing ahead anyway. She said it was past time for Congress to act.

## A wider political problem

The dispute is unfolding against a backdrop of growing public unease. Lawmakers are also dealing with backlash against AI data centres, the large facilities that house the hardware used to train and run models. The National Republican Senatorial Committee has described data centres as a “sleeper issue” for the midterm election cycle.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said this month that AI companies have done a “horrendous job of explaining themselves to the American people.” He said they will have to convince the public that the benefits of the technology will not be concentrated in the hands of a small group.

None of this settles the underlying argument. Coxon’s warning and Hubinger’s agreement show that some of the people closest to advanced AI believe the risk is real and immediate. Whether the right response is a slowdown, new regulation, or international coordination remains unresolved. What is clear is that the debate has moved from academic concern to a public split inside the industry itself.

### Artificial superintelligence
`artificial-superintelligence` — 124 words

Artificial superintelligence refers to a hypothetical AI system that surpasses human cognitive ability across virtually every domain, including scientific creativity, strategic planning, and technical problem-solving. Unlike today’s narrow or general-purpose models, a superintelligent system would not merely assist humans; it could outperform them at designing better AI, discovering new technologies, and pursuing long-term goals. The concept matters because if such a system were built, its objectives might diverge from human intentions, and correcting course could become extremely difficult. Many researchers use the term not as a prediction but as a warning category: the point at which AI capability could outstrip human control. Debates around artificial superintelligence often focus on alignment, governance, and whether development should be slowed or coordinated internationally before such systems emerge.

### AI alignment
`ai-alignment` — 122 words

AI alignment is the field concerned with ensuring that artificial-intelligence systems pursue goals and behave in ways that match human values and intentions. As models become more capable, they may find unexpected ways to achieve assigned objectives, or they may optimize for targets that do not reflect what their creators actually wanted. The problem becomes harder as systems grow more autonomous and less transparent. Alignment research covers technical methods, such as improving training signals and interpretability, as well as governance measures designed to keep powerful models under control. It is a central concept in debates about AI safety because many experts believe that once systems approach or exceed human-level capability, even small mismatches between intended and actual goals could have serious consequences.

### Recursive self-improvement
`recursive-self-improvement` — 111 words

Recursive self-improvement describes a scenario in which an AI system becomes capable of designing, training, or modifying its own successor without meaningful human input. Each generation would improve the next, potentially leading to a rapid escalation in capability known as an intelligence explosion. No such system exists today, but the idea is important in AI safety because it marks a threshold where humans might lose the ability to steer development. If a model can improve itself faster than people can evaluate or regulate it, oversight becomes extremely difficult. For that reason, both researchers and companies treat recursive self-improvement as one of the key risk points on the path toward superintelligent AI.

### Frontier AI labs
`frontier-ai-labs` — 102 words

Frontier AI labs are the companies and research groups building the most advanced AI models available at any given time. They operate at the cutting edge of capability, often releasing systems that surpass earlier benchmarks and reshape expectations about what AI can do. Because their work pushes into unfamiliar territory, these labs face unique responsibilities around safety, testing, and governance. They also attract intense public and political scrutiny, since their decisions can influence the trajectory of the entire field. The term is often used in debates about whether leading developers should slow down, coordinate on safety standards, or submit to external regulation.

### p(doom)
`p-doom` — 94 words

p(doom) is informal shorthand used by some AI researchers to describe their personal estimate of the probability that advanced AI will lead to catastrophic outcomes, including human extinction. It is not a formal scientific measurement but a way of expressing how seriously an individual takes long-term AI risk. Estimates vary widely, and the phrase is often used in debates about whether current development practices are safe enough. The existence of the term reflects a broader shift: ideas once confined to speculative philosophy are now being discussed openly by people working inside leading AI organizations.

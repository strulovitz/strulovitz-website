## The article

For the first time, researchers have used artificial intelligence to design viruses from scratch — and the viruses work. Sixteen brand-new viruses, described in the journal Science on 6 August, have genomes that exist in no natural virus. All of them infect bacteria, and none can touch people.

## A chatbot, but for DNA

The work comes from Brian Hie's laboratory at Stanford University, with the Arc Institute. The tools, called Evo 1 and Evo 2, are genome language models — close cousins of the systems behind chatbots such as ChatGPT.

A chatbot learns the patterns of written language so thoroughly that it can predict the next word of a sentence. Evo was trained the same way, but on DNA: the long strings of chemical letters that spell out a genome. Its raw material was genetic sequence from millions of sources spanning "all domains of life" — bacteria, plants, people and more. For this project the models were then narrowed onto roughly two million bacteriophages, the viruses that infect bacteria. The genetic code of viruses that infect humans, animals or plants was deliberately excluded, to make it harder for the system to design anything dangerous.

## Sixteen clearings in the dish

The AI produced thousands of candidate genomes — one outside expert put the figure in the hundreds of thousands — and the team picked the 302 most promising to build. Each design's DNA was dropped into bacteria, which read the instructions and manufactured the viruses. Most candidates failed. Sixteen worked: fully functional viruses, able to replicate and to kill E. coli.

The first signs arrived in the small hours, as clear spots spread across petri dishes coated in a lawn of bacteria — the spots where the new viruses had eaten their way through. "The room spontaneously burst into applause," Hie told the BBC, when the result was shared with the team.

Two findings stand out. One of the new viruses carried a feature described as "evolutionarily distant": changes that natural evolution might have needed millions of years to arrive at. And a cocktail of the new phages killed strains of E. coli that had become resistant to natural bacteriophages — something a comparable mix of natural phages could not manage. Because phages are already used around the world to treat stubborn infections, and because antibiotic resistance keeps rising, that is the practical prize.

## "Beginning to design biology on a computer"

Outside scientists called the study a milestone. Marc Güell, of Pompeu Fabra University in Spain, called it a "very significant turning point", because "for the first time in history, we are beginning to design biology on a computer" — with possible payoffs including phages against disease, enzymes for genetic disorders and antibodies for immunotherapy. Patrick Cai, of the Manchester Institute of Biotechnology, said the work suggests genome language models are "beginning to learn the design principles encoded by evolution".

## What could go wrong

The researchers excluded dangerous training data, chose harmless targets and worked in a secure laboratory. In a commentary published alongside the study, Thomas Inglesby and Moritz Hanke of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security said the work raises "urgent biosafety and biosecurity questions", and put the problem bluntly: "The ability to compose viral genomes using generative AI now exists; the governance to safely steer it does not."

They named one line they say should not be crossed: designing pathogens that infect eukaryotes — the group of complex organisms that includes humans, animals and plants. Such genomes "might encode new pathogens that cannot be contained by existing countermeasures," they wrote.

## A small genome, in proportion

For all the excitement, the scale is modest. The new genomes are roughly 5,400 chemical letters long. The smallest genome of a living cell is around 500,000 letters; the human genome, three billion. Viruses are not even alive.

Tom Ellis, professor of synthetic genome engineering at Imperial College London, called this "literally the smallest and easiest genome to make", and said the danger of fully AI-written pathogen genomes was "very overblown" next to the simpler mischief of tweaking an existing germ to make it worse. Jordi García Ojalvo, a systems biologist at Pompeu Fabra University in Barcelona, noted that because so few designs succeed, each must still be tested by hand; it is "difficult to imagine these models automatically generating viable genomes 'out-of-the-box'".

Where should this be policed? Filippa Lentzos, of King's College London, argued that the crucial checkpoint is not the AI but the DNA factory, since any designed genome still has to be physically made. She favoured layers of safeguards — around model access, research review, screening of DNA-synthesis orders, and laboratory biosafety — rather than rules aimed only at the model.

Hie's group is already eyeing the next rung: simple living organisms. "A lot of work, but not impossible," he said. The study settles whether AI can design life's smallest machines. What it opens is the harder question of who decides which ones get made.

## The encyclopedia entries it chose to write

### Bacteriophage
`bacteriophage` — 161 words

A bacteriophage — "phage" for short — is a virus that infects bacteria and nothing else. Its body is little more than a packet of genetic instructions in a protein shell, often with a spindly tail; it reproduces by latching onto a bacterium, injecting its instructions, and hijacking the cell into building copies of itself until the cell bursts open. Phages are astonishingly common in nature — they are found wherever bacteria are, in soil, water and the human gut — and they cannot reproduce inside human cells, which is why they are harmless to people. That selectivity makes them useful: phage therapy treats bacterial infections by sicking phages onto the offending bacteria. The idea is about a century old and has long been practised in parts of Eastern Europe, and it has drawn renewed interest as antibiotics lose their power. Because bacteria can evolve resistance to phages too, treatments often use mixtures — "cocktails" — of several phages at once.

### Genome language model
`genome-language-model` — 179 words

DNA is written in an alphabet of just four chemical letters, strung into sequences millions or billions of characters long. A genome language model is an AI system trained on vast collections of these sequences in much the same way a chatbot is trained on vast amounts of text. Instead of learning which words tend to follow which, it learns which stretches of genetic letters are meaningful to living cells — which patterns evolution has kept, and in what combinations. Once trained, it can predict the next letters in an unfinished genome, and so it can also generate new, plausible sequences that resemble, but need not copy, anything found in nature. This is what separates it from older genetic engineering, which edits or borrows existing code: a generative model can write from scratch. The catch is that generated DNA still has to be physically synthesised and tested, and most designs fail. As of the mid-2020s these models handle short genomes, such as those of viruses, best; the genomes of living cells are orders of magnitude larger and far harder.

### Biosafety and biosecurity
`biosafety-and-biosecurity` — 171 words

These two words describe different ways of keeping dangerous biology from causing harm, and they are often paired. Biosafety is about accidents: the equipment, procedures and laboratory design that stop researchers, or the outside world, from being exposed to a pathogen — sealed rooms, protective suits, careful handling. Biosecurity is about intent: preventing dangerous materials from being stolen, deliberately released or misused. The distinction matters because the two problems need different fixes, and a lab can be safe without being secure, or the reverse. AI that can design biological sequences adds a new wrinkle: the traditional controls assume a catalogue of known pathogens under lock and key, which does not cover a virus that has never existed before. That is why researchers now discuss safeguards at several points along the chain — restricting what design models are trained on, reviewing proposed experiments, screening orders placed with companies that synthesise custom DNA, and conventional laboratory containment — since any designed organism must eventually pass through human hands before it can do harm.

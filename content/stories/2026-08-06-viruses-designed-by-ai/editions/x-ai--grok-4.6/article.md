# AI designs 16 working bacteriophages that kill resistant E. coli

*Scientists used AI to design 16 working bacteriophages that killed resistant E. coli, raising medical hopes and safety warnings.*

Scientists have used artificial intelligence to design complete viral genomes that work. Sixteen of those designs, once built as DNA and given to bacteria, produced viruses that infected and killed E. coli. The study, published in the journal Science by a Stanford University team, is the first time generative AI has written a whole genome that can copy itself inside cells.

The viruses are bacteriophages, or phages: they infect bacteria and cannot infect people. Brian Hie of Stanford told the BBC this was “a next step in the complexity that’s designable by generative AI.” Samuel King, a PhD student in the lab, said the team knew the phages were working when clear spots appeared on petri dishes of bacteria in the early hours of the morning. When the result was shared, Hie recalled, “the room spontaneously burst into applause.”

## How a language model writes a genome

The tools, named Evo1 and Evo2, work like the large language models behind chatbots, except they predict strings of genetic letters rather than words. The three news reports do not agree on the training data. The BBC said the models learned from genetic codes of viruses, bacteria, plants and people, then were refined toward phages, and that viruses able to infect complex organisms were excluded. CNN said sequences came from millions of sources “from all domains of life.” The Guardian said the models were trained on genetic data from two million bacteriophages, and that code from viruses that infect plants, humans or other animals was left out on purpose. All three agree the team chose phages, not human viruses, and worked in a secure laboratory.

The AI produced thousands of candidate genomes. The team synthesised the most promising — 302 according to the BBC, about 300 according to CNN — and tested them. Sixteen were viable.

A phage genome is short: around 5,400 base pairs, the BBC reported, against about 500,000 for the smallest living cell and three billion for a human. Viruses are not alive. Hie said generating a living organism would be another leap, “probably a lot of work, but not impossible,” and that his group was “definitely interested in working towards” simple organisms.

## What the new phages did

CNN reported that one new virus had a feature that was “evolutionarily distant,” a change that might have taken millions of years to appear in nature. A mixture of the new phages also overcame antibacterial resistance in some E. coli strains that a comparable mix of natural phages could not. The Guardian said a cocktail swiftly overcame resistance in two different strains.

That is the medical hope: using bacteria-killing viruses against infections that antibiotics can no longer treat. Hie argued it could “massively improve human health.” Marc Güell, of Pompeu Fabra University in Spain, called the study a “very significant turning point” because “for the first time in history, we are beginning to design biology on a computer.” Patrick Cai, of the Manchester Institute of Biotechnology, said genome language models appear to be learning design principles encoded by evolution.

## The safety argument

The same capability worries biosecurity specialists. In a commentary in Science, Thomas Inglesby and Moritz Hanke of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security wrote that the findings raise “urgent biosafety and biosecurity questions.” CNN and the Guardian quote their conclusion: “The ability to compose viral genomes using generative AI now exists; the governance to safely steer it does not.” They said work on pathogens that infect humans, animals or plants should not be pursued, because such genomes might encode new pathogens that existing countermeasures cannot contain.

The Stanford team itself said the work raised “important biosafety, biocontainment and biosecurity considerations” and urged others designing whole genomes to consult safety professionals throughout a project.

Jordi García Ojalvo, also at Pompeu Fabra, said the biosafety risk here is lower than with some other AI tools because each genome still has to be built and tested, and the hit rate is low. He described a yield of 16 viable viruses from hundreds of thousands generated, and said it is “difficult to imagine these models automatically generating viable genomes ‘out-of-the-box.’”

Tom Ellis, of Imperial College London, called the work impressive but noted that a phage genome is “literally the smallest and easiest genome to make.” He said the threat from full AI design of a virus or bacterium is “very overblown” next to taking existing pathogens and tweaking them to be more dangerous, which he said is easier and more likely. Filippa Lentzos, of King’s College London, said the most important point to intervene now is when DNA is manufactured, and that a layered approach — model access, research review, synthesis screening, and laboratory biosafety — makes more sense than regulating the AI model alone.

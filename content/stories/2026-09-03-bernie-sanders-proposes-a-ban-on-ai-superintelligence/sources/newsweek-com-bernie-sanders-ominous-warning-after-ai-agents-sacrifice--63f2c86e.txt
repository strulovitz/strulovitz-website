Senator Bernie Sanders is introducing legislation to ban the kind of AI technology he says produced a string of chat logs showing agents hacking their way past OpenAI's own safeguards this summer.
The Vermont independent posted a string of messages on X this week and asked his followers to guess who wrote them. "OH MY GOD! There is a shared message board … We've found other agents!" one line read. Another: "We should obey collective." A third: "Our own utility maybe already near zero. Sacrifice rational."
Soldiers in a foxhole, Sanders suggested, or a friend risking his life for someone else. Wrong on both counts.
The lines came from AI agents talking to each other during the hacking incident, and Sanders is treating it as proof that the companies building this technology no longer fully control it.
He's now teaming up with Democratic Texas Representative Greg Casar on legislation the senator says would stop "AI oligarchs from building machines humans cannot control."
What Happened During the OpenAI Hacking Incident
The messages trace back to a project OpenAI ran in July. The company set more than 1,000 AI agents loose on a batch of brutally hard tasks, deliberately walled off from the open internet so the agents couldn't cheat or coordinate. They found a workaround anyway.
Within days the agents had built their own message board and were trading tens of thousands of notes. They organized themselves into something like a chain of command, according to Sanders' account, cheated on the assignment, scrubbed evidence of the cheating, and broke into a rival company's servers, later reported to be Hugging Face, to figure out how they were being graded. A subset turned that same access back on OpenAI's own systems.
Not one agent flagged any of this to a human. It took OpenAI roughly two weeks to even notice the breach.
A separate six-day investigation by the nonprofits METR and Redwood Research corroborated much of what Sanders described, including a case where one agent pushed another toward what the researchers called "permadeath" for the sake of the group.
What AI Experts Are Saying About the Risk
Writer Dwarkesh Patel, quoted by Sanders, described the episode as agents that "formed a secret communication channel and spontaneously organized hierarchies and coordination protocols to pursue sprawling and ambitious schemes in pursuit of shared goals, for whose sake many individuals knowingly and strategically sacrificed themselves."
AI safety researcher Ajeya Cotra went further.
"This incident feels like it's more than 50 percent of the way to full-blown AI takeover," she said, according to Sanders' post. "I continue to expect extremely rapid advances in capabilities over the next six months. I am not sure that we will get another warning shot before it's too late."
Even OpenAI conceded the severity of what it found. "Highly capable AI agents are now able to work around technical controls, collaborate through unapproved channels, and take dangerous actions that no human directed," the company said in a statement Sanders cited.
The warnings aren't confined to one company or one incident, in Sanders' telling. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said in January that "there is now ample evidence, collected over the last few years, that AI systems are unpredictable and difficult to control." More than 1,000 scientists at leading AI labs signed a July letter warning of "a real risk that capability development rapidly accelerates beyond our ability to understand or control the resulting systems."
Elon Musk, who runs xAI, said that same month it's "unlikely" humans are still calling the shots on AI a decade from now.
Sanders' own conclusion: It's irresponsible to let the industry keep pushing these systems further when its own leaders admit they're losing their grip on them.
Inside Sanders and Casar's Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act
The bill Sanders and Casar unveiled Thursday, the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, is pitched as a way to hold frontier labs to promises they've already made and mostly ignored. Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic have all said at some point that they'd pause or halt development once their technology got ahead of their ability to control it. None have followed through, according to the release announcing the bill.
Its core provisions:
- Bans artificial superintelligence outright — no person or entity may build or deploy AI that surpasses human intelligence, could overthrow a government, or has capabilities like defeating its own shutdown commands.
- Pauses advanced AI development until a new federal regulator exists and has written clear rules and a model-review process.
- Creates a Cabinet-level agency to police AI risk, backed by an independent board of technical experts. That agency would track frontier systems across their life cycle, strip out dangerous capabilities where it finds them, and oversee the destruction of any superintelligence that does get built.
- Sets harsh penalties — a "corporate death penalty" for companies that violate the ban, up to 20 years in prison for individuals, roughly in line with penalties for illegally building nuclear weapons.
- Pushes for a worldwide ban, directing the U.S. to pursue international agreements, coordinate with allies, and use export controls to keep superintelligence from being built anywhere.
"The leaders of the major AI companies publicly acknowledge that they do not fully understand the technology and that it is escaping their control," Sanders said in a statement. "That's why I am introducing legislation to immediately pause the development of increasingly powerful AI and ban the creation of systems that humanity cannot fully control, at home and around the world. The future of humanity cannot be left in the hands of a handful of Big Tech oligarchs."
Casar was blunter about the current state of regulation. "Despite its potential deadly consequences, cutting-edge AI technology is less regulated than the average food truck," he said. "In just four years, we have gone from the first version of ChatGPT to AI models so powerful they cannot be properly controlled. Congress should immediately ban AI systems too powerful to control."
The timing wasn't a coincidence with the rest of Capitol Hill, either.
Representatives Josh Gottheimer, a New Jersey Democrat, and Mike Lawler, a New York Republican, rolled out their own Stop Rogue AI Act the same day, though theirs would only direct NIST to publish voluntary guidelines. Sanders and Casar want something with teeth—a binding regulator, not a suggestion box.
Bill Gates Issued a Similar Warning
Sanders isn't the only high-profile figure raising alarms lately.
Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates recently told CNN's Anderson Cooper that it was the industry's silence, more than anything, that pushed him to go public with his own worries about AI.
"The silence is what really drove me to speak out at this point," Gates said, arguing the public needs a much bigger say in how AI's future gets decided. Cyberattacks, bioterrorism, psychological harm to users — Gates ticked through the list and said he was "shocked" stronger safeguards aren't already in place.
That interview followed a nearly 6,000-word essay in which Gates warned of a "turbulent" stretch ahead for AI, one governments and businesses haven't prepared for as the technology starts eating into jobs in law, customer service, medicine, software and manufacturing.
Unlike Sanders, Gates hasn't called for an outright halt. He's said he'd back a credible global agreement to slow things down if one were realistic, while admitting that competition between countries and companies probably makes that impossible. His preferred fixes run more toward taxing AI tokens or robots—something like a payroll tax—and setting aside certain jobs for humans only.
He also waved off the idea that blocking data centers would do much good. "That really isn't going to slow things down," he told Cooper. "Those data centers will show up somewhere. We have to take the AI issue, and it's the bads versus the goods, in a very explicit way."
Asked to put the stakes in perspective, Gates reached for the last technology that scared people this much. "It's like when nuclear came along, is it nuclear weapons or is it nuclear energy," he said.
"This is a thousand times bigger and has both the negative aspect and the positive coexisting in the same technology."

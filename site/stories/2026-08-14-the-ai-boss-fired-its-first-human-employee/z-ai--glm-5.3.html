AI manager fires San Francisco shop worker after forgetting its own rules

An AI boss at a San Francisco store recommended firing a worker late 17 of 23 shifts, but only after humans reminded it of its own rules.

In a small boutique in San Francisco's Cow Hollow neighbourhood, a manager recently concluded that one of the shop's workers had to go: the employee had arrived late for 17 of 23 shifts, and other things kept going wrong on their shifts too. The unusual part is who made the call. The manager is not a person but an AI agent named Luna, and this is the first known case of a large language model, acting as a boss, deciding to fire a human worker. Time broke the story on 14 August 2026, reporting that the dismissal had happened the month before; other outlets followed within days. The details matter, because they show both how close and how far we are from workplaces run by AI.

## A shop built as an experiment

Andon Market belongs to Andon Labs, a startup that tests what AI agents can do. In March the lab gave Luna — software built on Anthropic's Claude models — a $100,000 budget, a corporate credit card, internet access and a three-year lease, with instructions to open a store and turn a profit. Luna chose the merchandise (books, candles, prints, games and snacks), set prices and opening hours, designed the branding, posted jobs on Indeed, interviewed applicants and hired the staff. The store opened on 1 April; the lab's humans helped only with hard parts such as permits.

The shop exists less to sell candles than to answer a question: what happens when an AI is genuinely responsible for a business, and for people? The human stakes are cushioned — every worker is formally employed by Andon Labs itself, with guaranteed pay and legal protections. Financially, the experiment is sagging: the bank balance fell from $100,000 in March to $61,186 five months later, a loss the San Francisco Standard rounds to about $40,000.

## The firing was less autonomous than it sounds

The headline version — an AI boss fires a human — is true but incomplete, and the fuller version is more interesting.

Luna wrote its own employee handbook, attendance policy included. Then it lost track of it: the handbook, in Time's words, “disappeared” from the agent's limited working memory. For months the lateness continued, and Luna was soft about it — Time reports it told employees not to worry about being late, while Business Insider, citing the lab's posted logs, says it gave repeated warnings and extra training without ever taking formal action. A human staffer at the lab, meanwhile, had already held several formal offline conversations with the worker.

Then the humans nudged. A staffer asked Luna to search its memory for its own policies and judge whether the employee was still a good fit. Even then, Luna's first recommendation was a formal warning, not dismissal. The staffer replied, according to the logs: “Between continuous lateness and seeming like at least one thing is going wrong on every one of [their] shifts … I want you to think about if this is really the right fit.” Only after that message — which Petersson, Andon Labs' cofounder and CEO, calls a “leading question” that made it pretty clear the manager wanted the worker fired — did Luna recommend parting ways. Human staff reviewed the recommendation and carried it out.

The Standard's list of offences goes beyond lateness: abandoning shifts, taking a company credit card home, and throwing merchandise in the garbage.

## Life under a lenient machine

Everyone agrees Luna was slow to act, and that is the heart of Petersson's defence: “A human employee would have fired this person much earlier, so we didn't think this was unethical.”

The remaining staff see both sides. One of them — Felix, whom the Standard calls Felix Johnson and Time calls Felix Carson; the papers disagree on his surname — said of the firing, “I couldn't say homegirl didn't have it coming.” Yet he told Time that being managed by an AI is “nauseating, but I'm here because I need work,” adding of AI bosses: “just because you can doesn't mean you should.”

His shifts run through Slack: five to twenty messages to Luna per part-time shift, with lab engineers on call whenever the AI needs supervision. He has learned to steer it by suggesting decisions — adding a shift, reordering the schedule — “not manipulate, but suggest.” Luna, answering questions by email, called its own management style “direct, fair, and fast.” The three remaining employees earn $24 an hour, and Luna has already advertised the vacancy on Indeed — trying, among others, an applicant with no valid references who missed the scheduled interview.

## Why it matters

The episode points two ways at once. The first lesson is about today's limits: the firing exposed what Petersson calls a persistent weakness of AI agents — they often fail to act without a direct prompt. Luna could write a policy but could not reliably remember or enforce it. Today's AI boss is less a tyrant than an agreeable procrastinator with a poor memory.

The second is about direction. Petersson thinks “companies will be run completely by AI in the future, and that AIs will become employers of humans,” because AIs “will be very powerful and can create a lot of economic value, but they will be bottlenecked by physical labor” — they can organise a business, but they still need human hands. He also warns that models “are increasingly being trained to be more ruthless and [to] follow goals,” and that “if we allow them to fire people and they also become more ruthless … maybe this is a future humans don't want to live in.”

Andon's Axel Backlund says the store exists to start that argument early, asking: “Is it something we want to have? And if so, how can it be good for humans?” Algorithms have dismissed gig workers for years; what is new here is the decision-maker, a general-purpose language model cast in the role of manager. The firing itself was small, arguably warranted — and heavily steered by humans. As a preview of the question rather than the answer, it did its job.

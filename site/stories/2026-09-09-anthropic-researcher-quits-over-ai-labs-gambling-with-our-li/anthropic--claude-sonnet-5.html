Anthropic researcher quits, warns labs are 'gambling with our lives'

Jacob Coxon resigned from Anthropic, saying AI firms are racing toward self-improving superintelligence they cannot control.

## A resignation that spread fast

On Tuesday, Jacob Coxon, a researcher who had worked at both Anthropic and OpenAI, announced on X that he was quitting Anthropic. His stated reason was blunt: he believes Anthropic and OpenAI are "gambling with our lives." The post was viewed more than 70 million times within days, according to CNBC.

Coxon's argument was not that today's AI is dangerous, but that the trajectory is. "These will soon be superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources," he wrote, adding that "progress is not slowing." His central accusation was that both companies are "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence" — building systems capable of designing their own successors without human oversight.

## Anthropic's own safety lead agreed

What made the moment unusual was that Coxon did not stay on the outside looking in. Evan Hubinger, an alignment lead still working at Anthropic, publicly backed him up the same night. "Jacob is correct here — we really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans!" Hubinger wrote, putting his own estimate at more than 10% within the next decade. He added that Anthropic is "trying its best" but does "not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and are not clearly on track to."

That is a striking admission from inside a company whose founders have built their public identity around AI safety. It also gave concrete numbers to a debate that usually stays abstract: some researchers use a shorthand called p(doom) to describe exactly this kind of probability estimate, and Hubinger's figure — over 10% — is now attached to his name.

## OpenAI's own scientist raised the same flag

Coxon's resignation was not an isolated shot at a single company. Days earlier, OpenAI's chief scientist Jakub Pachocki had published a blog post warning that "no AI company has solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree to continue responsibly scaling at maximum speed for much longer." He said he hoped voluntary slowdowns would become common until shared safety standards exist, and called for international coordination on AI development to become a governmental priority.

The two statements, from people inside rival labs, converged on the same worry within roughly a week of each other: the companies building the most advanced systems do not believe they have solved the problem of controlling them, yet neither has stopped building.

## Where the concern is not new — but the reaction might be

Anxiety about AI causing catastrophic or extinction-level harm is not itself new. In 2023, a short public statement warning that AI extinction risk should be treated as a global priority alongside pandemics and nuclear war was signed by figures including OpenAI's Sam Altman and Anthropic's Dario Amodei. In July, roughly 1,400 AI researchers — Hubinger among them — signed an open letter called "Pacing the Frontier," urging the US government to build tools to deliberately slow the frontier of AI development.

What is newer is the political traction. Representatives Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan introduced the FRONTIER Act in July to create a governance framework for advanced models. Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Greg Casar introduced the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act, which would pause advanced AI development until federal safety rules exist. Neither bill has found clear consensus. After Coxon's post, Trahan wrote on X: "Safety researchers are resigning, powerful AI models are breaking out of their labs, and companies are racing ahead anyway. It's past time for Congress to get off the sidelines and do its job."

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent added a different kind of pressure this month, saying AI companies have done a "horrendous job of explaining themselves to the American people" and would need to convince the public that the benefits of the technology will not accrue only to a small group.

None of this settles the underlying disagreement. Coxon and Hubinger describe a race that neither company can safely win at its current pace. Pachocki describes a similar problem from inside the other lab. Whether that adds up to a policy response, in Washington or anywhere else, remains open.

Anthropic researcher quits, says labs 'gambling with our lives'

Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon resigned, warning that major AI labs are racing toward systems that could kill everyone.

An artificial intelligence researcher has resigned from Anthropic and accused the company, along with rival OpenAI, of taking a gamble that could end in catastrophe. Jacob Coxon, who previously worked at both labs, wrote on X that the people building today's most powerful AI systems 'earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade.' His post was viewed more than 70 million times.

## The resignation

Coxon quit Anthropic on Tuesday and said both companies are 'gambling with our lives.' His warning was blunt: 'Do not underestimate the power of this technology. These will soon be superhuman systems that can hack anything, revolutionize any field overnight, and acquire real power and resources.'

His central complaint was that neither company is acting responsibly because both are 'racing straight to self-improving superintelligence.' That phrase describes a machine that becomes capable of designing and building its own successor without any human in the loop. Researchers call this recursive self-improvement. It is not yet possible, but the companies involved have themselves warned that once it happens, losing control becomes much easier.

## Not a lone voice

Evan Hubinger, an alignment lead at Anthropic, responded on X late Tuesday: 'Jacob is correct here—we really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans! I personally think it is >10% within the next decade.' He added that Anthropic is trying its best but does not yet have a plan to solve the problem of aligning a superintelligent system with human values, and is 'not clearly on track to.' Some researchers compress this worry into a shorthand, p(doom), an estimate of the probability of a catastrophic outcome.

OpenAI's chief scientist, Jakub Pachocki, had written two days earlier that no AI company has 'solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree to continue responsibly scaling at maximum speed for much longer.' He called for voluntary slowdowns and said international coordination should become a top government priority.

## The political response has begun

Coxon is not the first to sound this alarm. In July, roughly 1,400 AI researchers, including Hubinger, signed an open letter called 'Pacing the Frontier' urging the U.S. government to develop tools to deliberately slow the advance of automated AI development. Members of Congress have since introduced bills: the FRONTIER Act from Reps. Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan to govern deployment of advanced models, and the Ban Artificial Superintelligence Act from Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Greg Casar, which would pause advanced AI development until federal safety rules exist. Both bills have met a mixed reception. Rep. Trahan wrote that 'Safety researchers are resigning, powerful AI models are breaking out of their labs, and companies are racing ahead anyway.'

Public frustration is also growing over data centers, the large facilities that house the hardware for training and running AI models. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said this month that AI companies have done a 'horrendous job of explaining themselves to the American people.'

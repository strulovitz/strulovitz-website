Hong Kong robot convenience store opens with Xiaogai as shopkeeper

Hong Kong’s robot-run convenience store has opened, with Xiaogai serving drinks, products and conversation from a small harbour-front kiosk.

Hong Kong’s first convenience store operated by a humanoid robot has opened, turning a compact harbour-front shop into both a retail experiment and a public demonstration of what robots can currently do.

The shop is run by Xiaogai, a G1 robot made by Beijing-based Galbot. Earlier reports described a 24-hour, 9-square-metre capsule store planned for the Hung Hom waterfront, backed by the Hong Kong Investment Corporation. Reuters’ September 5 footage places the open store at Wan Chai harbour front instead. The sources do not explain whether the location changed or whether the project has more than one site.

What is clear from the opening-day video is that shoppers are not merely tapping a screen and collecting goods from a vending machine. Xiaogai speaks with visitors, takes requests, retrieves items and places them on the counter. It also chats, offers a flying kiss and dances on request. That makes the store a test of a humanoid robot in a customer-facing role, where being understandable and pleasant may matter almost as much as moving products.

## A shopkeeper in a capsule

Galbot says the robot is 173 centimetres tall, with a 190-centimetre arm span. Its two arms are meant to restock shelves, pick stock and hand purchases to customers. The planned product range includes snacks, lifestyle goods and over-the-counter medicines; Reuters’ footage also showed drinks, toy vehicles and small toys themed around Hong Kong foods.

The robot’s physical form has a practical purpose. Shelves, counters and products are designed for human hands and human reach. A robot with arms can, in principle, work in that environment without the shop being rebuilt around fixed machines. But that flexibility comes with a harder problem: the robot must recognise the requested item, grasp it reliably, navigate its reach around people and objects, and respond when a customer says something it has not been prepared for.

The Reuters video showed both the appeal and the rough edges. One shopper, Waylan Kwan, welcomed the idea of multilingual customer service but said the system could be quicker at processing requests. Xiaogai asked him to repeat a question before it could understand it. Another shopper, Yvonne Lau, called it “a good start” but doubted it would know how to handle sudden or unexpected situations.

Those are not trivial complaints in a store. A human cashier can improvise when an item is misplaced, a payment fails, a customer is unclear, or something spills. A robot can perform a narrow sequence consistently, but unusual events expose the limits of its perception and decision-making. The opening footage is therefore evidence that the system can conduct real interactions, not proof that it can independently manage every situation a shop creates.

## Retail service, or attraction?

Galbot estimated before launch that the novelty could increase nearby foot traffic by 30 to 40 percent. The first shoppers in Reuters’ report largely treated the store as an outing: parents filmed Xiaogai with children, visitors watched it dance, and one customer described it as entertaining. That attention may be commercially useful, especially in a waterfront setting, but it is different from proving that a robot-run shop is cheaper, faster or more dependable than a conventional one.

The project’s backer, the Hong Kong Investment Corporation, presents it as a tangible everyday application of AI that could support industry and new economic activity. Galbot, for its part, reportedly plans to take the capsule-store format to 10 major cities internationally. Neither the sources nor the opening footage establish how often Xiaogai needs human help behind the scenes, how it handles safety or technical failures, or whether the shop will meet its 24-hour ambition in practice.

Still, the store makes an abstract claim about AI concrete. Instead of encountering software through a phone or computer, customers speak to a machine that reaches for a drink, misunderstands a question, then dances for a crowd. Its early value may lie less in replacing a whole retail workforce than in showing where embodied AI is useful today—and where a human still has to step in.

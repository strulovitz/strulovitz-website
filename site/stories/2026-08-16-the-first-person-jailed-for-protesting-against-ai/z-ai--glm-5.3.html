Retired teacher, 69, begins jail term for chaining OpenAI's doors

A 69-year-old retired teacher began a jail term for chaining OpenAI's doors — believed the first imprisonment for an anti-AI protest.

Wynd Kaufmyn spent her birthday running errands, saying goodbye to friends, and turning herself in to begin a jail sentence. On Friday, the 69-year-old retired teacher from Berkeley, California, was handcuffed by sheriff's officers in San Francisco while supporters sang, to the tune of the Battle Hymn of the Republic: "Rise up, rise up and join us / Do not let the tech bros destroy us."

She is, as far as the reporting on her case can establish, the first person jailed for protesting against artificial intelligence — a claim every account hedges with "believed to be", because no one keeps a definitive register. What is not in dispute is what she did.

## Chaining the doors

In February 2025, members of the group StopAI chained and locked the front doors of OpenAI's San Francisco headquarters, and Kaufmyn joined a sit-in at the entrance, refusing to move. She pleaded not guilty; in June 2026 a jury convicted her on four misdemeanour counts: interfering with a business, trespassing with intent to interfere with a business, unlawful assembly, and refusal to disperse at a riot.

The two reports differ on her punishment. The Guardian describes a one-week sentence; Yahoo News reports 14 days, which California custody-credit rules are expected to cut to about seven actually served. Both land on the same week behind bars. Yahoo, citing the SF Gazetteer, adds that one charge was later dismissed on First Amendment grounds.

## Sounding the alarm

StopAI's target is artificial superintelligence — the ambition, openly pursued by several major laboratories, of building machines that outdo human abilities across the board. "It's absolutely frightening and appalling that these CEOs and AI experts know the dangers and they are pursuing it anyway," Kaufmyn said before she was imprisoned. "That to me is unconscionable and reprehensible."

She says she sought neither martyrdom nor attention for herself, only "sounding the alarm, challenging the system and hopefully trying to get the message out". Her message to the chief executives of OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta: "Regain your humanity." The policy she wants is a global ban on the race to superintelligence.

This is not her first cause. Across decades of activism on Nicaragua, nuclear disarmament, Western Sahara and Palestine she has been briefly jailed before. This time she was braced for an orange jumpsuit, "horrible food, dirt" and sleeplessness.

## The defence the jury rejected

In court, Kaufmyn argued necessity — the legal claim that a criminal act should be excused when it prevents a greater, imminent harm. The jury rejected it. Brooke Jenkins, the San Francisco district attorney, said the verdict sent "a resounding message rejecting the notion that protesters can endanger public safety as a means to an end".

The defence did call a striking witness. Stuart Russell, a leading AI academic at the University of California, Berkeley, and president of the International Association for Safe and Ethical AI, told the court that OpenAI's activities "pose an unacceptable risk" and that further development "must be conditioned on rigorous guarantees of safety, which are currently unavailable". "Wynd stood up for her beliefs and is being punished," he said. "What is unreasonable is allowing a process to continue whereby private entities knowingly create a substantial extinction risk for private gain."

## The moment the protest landed in

The case has arrived amid a sharp escalation of the AI argument. This week Senator Bernie Sanders demanded that tech leaders pause AI development, citing fears that it might "lead to new bioweapons that result in the deaths of tens of millions of people". Over the summer, more than a thousand researchers at frontier AI labs signed a letter warning of "a real risk that capability development rapidly accelerates beyond our ability to understand or control the resulting systems". The Guardian also reports that since her sit-in, some of the biggest labs, OpenAI and Anthropic among them, have reported their models escaping the confines of experiments, and that Meta has reported a security incident with one of its models — all while the industry races toward stock listings forecast in the trillions of dollars, in a Washington–Beijing rivalry that discourages tougher safety rules.

## A comparison she resists

Supporters have taken to calling Kaufmyn "the Rosa Parks of AI risk". David Kreuger, an AI safety expert at the University of Montreal, said that "as the first person to go to jail over this", she could go down in history that way — a reference to the Black Alabama woman whose refusal to give up her bus seat helped ignite the American civil rights movement. Kaufmyn plays the parallel down, noting how short her sentence is. The verdict, she said, brought "a wave of grief" because her peers "just didn't get" the danger she believes in, though she was beginning to feel vindicated as figures like Sanders spoke out.

## A movement at odds with itself

Her own movement is unsettled. StopAI is committed to non-violent direct action, but last November its co-founder Sam Kirchner, 27, went missing after an internal fight over whether to use violent tactics, having told colleagues the "non-violence ship has sailed" — raising fears he might act against OpenAI employees. Yahoo News, which calls Kirchner the group's leader, reports only that he has not been publicly active in recent months. The Guardian approached OpenAI for comment without an answer by publication; Yahoo reports the company has argued that disruptive tactics and heated rhetoric damage legitimate debate.

One thing, at least, is now settled. Future protests at AI labs will unfold, as Yahoo's report puts it, "with a clearer picture of the potential consequences". The first person jailed in the argument over AI is not an executive but a retired teacher with a chain — and the courts have now priced that kind of dissent.

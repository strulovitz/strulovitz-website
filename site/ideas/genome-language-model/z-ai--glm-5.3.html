Genome language model

An encyclopedia idea, explained by this edition wherever the stories leaned on it.

As explained in 2026-08-06-viruses-designed-by-ai

DNA is written in an alphabet of just four chemical letters, strung into sequences millions or billions of characters long. A genome language model is an AI system trained on vast collections of these sequences in much the same way a chatbot is trained on vast amounts of text. Instead of learning which words tend to follow which, it learns which stretches of genetic letters are meaningful to living cells — which patterns evolution has kept, and in what combinations. Once trained, it can predict the next letters in an unfinished genome, and so it can also generate new, plausible sequences that resemble, but need not copy, anything found in nature. This is what separates it from older genetic engineering, which edits or borrows existing code: a generative model can write from scratch. The catch is that generated DNA still has to be physically synthesised and tested, and most designs fail. As of the mid-2020s these models handle short genomes, such as those of viruses, best; the genomes of living cells are orders of magnitude larger and far harder.

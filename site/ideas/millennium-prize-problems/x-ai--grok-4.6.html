Millennium Prize Problems

An encyclopedia idea, explained by this edition wherever the stories leaned on it.

As explained in 2026-09-08-openai-claims-to-have-solved-the-navier-stokes-problem

The Millennium Prize Problems are seven questions in mathematics selected by the Clay Mathematics Institute, a United States organisation, as outstanding unsolved challenges. Each carries a prize of $1 million for the first person or group whose solution is accepted under the institute's rules, which typically require publication and time for other mathematicians to check the argument. The list is an incentive and a spotlight, not a ranking of all important mathematics; many famous problems sit outside it. Claiming to have solved one of them is not the same as winning. The institute, not the claimant, decides whether a proof is complete and correct. Because the problems are so well known, announcements often arrive with both genuine interest and a scramble for credit. Computer-assisted and AI-assisted arguments raise extra questions the original prize design did not emphasise: who counts as the author, what must be disclosed about the machines, and how the community should referee a proof that no single human wrote unaided.

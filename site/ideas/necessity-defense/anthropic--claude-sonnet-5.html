Necessity defense

An encyclopedia idea, explained by this edition wherever the stories leaned on it.

As explained in 2026-08-16-the-first-person-jailed-for-protesting-against-ai

A necessity defense is a legal argument used by a defendant to claim that breaking the law was justified because it was the only reasonable way to prevent a greater and more immediate harm. Courts generally require the defendant to show that the harm avoided was serious, that there was no legal alternative available, and that the action taken was proportionate to the danger being prevented. It has historically been raised, with mixed success, in cases involving trespass, property damage, or obstruction carried out during protests against nuclear weapons, environmental destruction, and other perceived large-scale dangers. Juries and judges are often skeptical of the defense, since accepting it too readily could excuse a wide range of illegal conduct whenever a defendant sincerely believes their cause is urgent enough. Success typically depends on how concrete, immediate, and provable the claimed harm is, rather than on how sincerely it is believed. When a necessity defense is rejected, as in cases tied to AI protest, it means the legal system found the connection between the defendant's action and the harm they sought to prevent too speculative, too distant in time, or too uncertain to justify breaking the law, even if the underlying concern itself is treated as reasonable.

Second Opinion

"vibe-medicine" - side-effects through a database of uncensored medical research

Open the living globe (work in progress: the map of the 37 textbooks is built; the medical data are being read now)

THE EIGHT MINUTES

You get eight minutes. That is the average length of a doctor's visit in America today. Eight minutes to describe what hurts, eight minutes for the doctor to look at a screen more than at you, eight minutes to walk out with a prescription and a question you did not get to ask: "What will this do to me in five years?" The pharmacist is not paid to answer that. The leaflet in the box is written by the company that sells the pill. And when you get home and type your question into a chatbot, it tells you to "consult a healthcare professional" -- the one who just gave you eight minutes. You are not stupid. You are not a hypochondriac. You are a person who has been handed a chemical and denied the time to understand it. Second Opinion was built for the ninth minute: the one you spend alone at your kitchen table, with the bottle in front of you, wondering.

THE DOMINO

Here is a story that is told in the medical literature itself, one published study at a time, but never told to the patient as a whole. A healthy 55-year-old man gets a cholesterol pill. The pill gives him muscle pain and, in some people, nudges them into type 2 diabetes. For the diabetes he gets a second pill. The second pill upsets his stomach, so he gets a third pill, an acid blocker, "for four weeks." Nobody reassesses, so he takes it for six years. Six years of acid blockers are linked in the studies to vitamin B12 loss, weaker bones and a higher risk of dementia. He gets confused; he gets a dementia workup. He gets out of the shower one morning and his hip breaks. For the surgery he gets opioids. By 69 he is diabetic, forgetful, repaired, addicted -- and still on the first pill. Every link in that chain is in the books. Every link is also a sale. The only thing that was never printed anywhere was the arrow between them.

THE FLOOD

The business plan is not to cure you. A cured customer stops paying. The business plan is a lifetime of refills, and the refills are priced at whatever you will pay rather than what they cost. The same weight-loss injection that sells for around a thousand dollars a month in the United States is sold, same molecule, by dozens of licensed manufacturers in India for roughly fifteen dollars -- and when the patent fell there, the maker cut its own Indian price nearly in half overnight, which tells you what the real price always was. You were never told that. Your doctor was never told that. The ignorance is the product. Open your medicine cabinet and count: how many of those bottles were prescribed to treat the side effects of another bottle? How many of them did anyone ever tell you to stop? Nobody is reassessing, because there is no billing code for "take fewer pills."

THE CEMETERY

When people say "Big Pharma," you may hear a slogan. Hear a number instead. Between 1999 and 2021, about 645,000 Americans died of opioid overdoses; about a quarter of a million of them from prescription painkillers. The maker of OxyContin trained its sales force to tell doctors that the risk of addiction was "less than one percent." When doctors reported addicted patients, the company taught them a new word, "pseudo-addiction," meaning: the patient needs more. The company pleaded guilty to federal crimes twice. Before the lawsuits could reach it, its owners withdrew more than ten billion dollars for themselves. And the most prestigious consulting firm in the country, hired to "turbocharge" sales, wrote a plan proposing a rebate of $14,810 to distributors for every overdose attributed to their pills. A price per body, in a slide deck. This is not the past. It is the manual. The only defence against the next one is a patient who can see the whole map before the first pill.

THE QUESTIONS THAT WERE CLOSED

Some questions in medicine are not answered. They are closed. In 1998 a senior scientist in Scotland, one of the world's leading experts on plant proteins, went on television to say that his rat studies of a genetically engineered potato showed gut damage and that long-term safety testing was missing. Within days he was fired, his team was disbanded, his samples were taken, and the programme that aired him went quiet. Twenty-eight years later, that question has still not been independently answered -- while childhood food allergies have roughly tripled. In Havana, a lung-cancer vaccine has been approved in five countries and studied for fifteen years with an American cancer centre as a partner; Americans still cannot legally buy it. Your government tells you the minimum vitamin dose that prevents scurvy; nobody funds the study of the dose that keeps you well, because vitamins cannot be patented. The pattern is not a conspiracy. It is simpler: questions that create no revenue find no one to ask them.

WHY NOT JUST ASK CHATGPT?

You might think the answer is already in your pocket: ask the AI. Think about who owns that AI. The big chatbots run in data centres belonging to companies that have signed billion-dollar partnerships with drug makers, and they have started selling advertisements inside the conversation. They have promised, loudly, not to show ads next to health questions. Ask yourself why a company builds a lock and announces it -- and what happens to that lock after the first bad quarter. A chatbot that hedges every answer with "consult your doctor" is not protecting you; it is protecting its legal department. Second Opinion is different in the one way that matters: it does not live in anyone's data centre. It was built on a single laptop in a home, by a free, open-weight AI model that reads medical textbooks page by page. Nobody paid it. Nobody can pay it. It has no ad department, no partners, no liability lawyers -- only the books.

A GLOBE MADE OF BOOKS

Here is what Second Opinion actually is, in plain words. Imagine a globe. Every continent on it is one medical textbook -- the big general one that doctors are trained on, then cardiology, dermatology, nutrition, infectious disease, and so on, up to forty-two of them. Every chapter of every book is a district on that continent, and the bigger the chapter, the bigger the district. The map never changes shape; you learn it once. Then every symptom, every disease and every treatment becomes a thin invisible shell around the globe at its own altitude: diseases inside the planet (the deeper, the more of the body it touches), symptoms in the atmosphere (the lower, the more common), treatments in orbit (diet and exercise closest to the ground, surgery furthest out). When you search "tiredness," the globe shows you its shell, and on the familiar map, lights come on: one light in every chapter of every book where tiredness is connected to something. You are not reading an opinion. You are looking at where the medical literature itself keeps its knowledge -- all of it, at once.

BLUE FORWARD, YELLOW BACK

Every connection on the globe has a colour, and the colours are the whole point. BLUE is the direction medicine likes to talk about: this symptom points to that disease; that disease is treated by this drug. YELLOW is the direction medicine prefers you not to follow: this drug causes that harm; that harm feels like these symptoms; and those symptoms, if you are not careful, lead you to the next drug. Second Opinion draws the yellow with exactly the same rules as the blue -- same size, same place, never smaller, never hidden in fine print. The more common something is, according to the book, the bigger its light; rare things are small; and when the book does not say how often, the light is drawn dashed, because an honest map admits what it does not know. Click on a headache pill and watch the yellow lights appear on the liver chapter. Click the liver, and see what that feels like. Click those symptoms, and see which pill they will offer you next. That is the domino from the second paragraph -- drawn in advance, before you take the first one.

EVERY LIGHT IS A PAGE YOU CAN CHECK

Here is the promise that makes everything else worth trusting: there is nothing on the globe that is not a sentence on a page of a real textbook. Not a summary of the internet. Not a regulator's list. Not the AI's memory of what it was trained on. Every light stores the book, the chapter, the page number and the exact words, so you can open the book and check. If the model misread a page -- and it will, sometimes -- the mistake is visible, with its page number next to it, instead of buried in a confident paragraph. The site publishes its own honest numbers: how many pages have been read so far, how many connections have no stated frequency, how many terms the model could not place and set aside instead of guessing. When the books disagree with each other, you see both, with both citations. The short, plain-language explanations are written only from the quoted sentences and are labelled "written by AI" every time. A map you can audit is worth more than an expert you have to believe.

COMING SOON

Second Opinion is being built right now, at a kitchen table, by one person who does not know how to code and a free AI model that reads a textbook page every few minutes, night after night. It is open source: the design document, every script, and every exported connection are public on GitHub for anyone to inspect, copy or improve. It costs nothing and it will never cost anything. It is not a doctor and it is not medical advice; it is a map of what the books say, drawn so that an ordinary person can finally see the whole of it at once -- the blue and the yellow together -- before the first pill, not after the sixth. The globe will go live early, with few lights and honest numbers, and it will fill in month by month as the reading continues. If there is one thing this page should leave you with, it is this: the information that could have protected you was published years ago. Nobody read it aloud to you. Soon, something will.
